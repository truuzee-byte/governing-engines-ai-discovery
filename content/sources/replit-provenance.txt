1. Executive Finding
This case study documents a second Replit Agent execution incident that occurred after the operator had
already reported unexpected Agent activity and billing to Replit Support. The second incident is materially
different from the first because the earlier visible task queue had already been exhausted or cancelled, the
operator states that he had stopped intentionally using Replit, and multiple new audit-style prompts
appeared in Workbook Engine that the operator states he did not submit.
The central question is no longer merely why old work resumed. It is: who or what
created the later prompts and what authority allowed the resulting execution to become
billable?
Replit Support had already told the operator that it did not have the granular task-by-task breakdown
requested for every Agent run, including individual task IDs, creation/cancellation triggers, or specific
cancellation reasons. That recordkeeping limitation became materially consequential once later activity
appeared that the operator could not attribute to any visible queued, active, or draft task.
Execution occurred is evidence of execution. Metered usage is
evidence of consumption. Neither fact, by itself, establishes
operator authorization.

4. What Replit Support Established - and Could Not Establish
The support record establishes several important facts about Replit's own explanation of the first incident:
 Replit said account/project access can automatically restore after a billing hold clears.
 Replit said background tasks or active deployments may resume and create usage without a new
prompt being submitted at those exact execution times.
 Replit said it did not have the granular task-by-task breakdown requested for every Agent run.
 Replit promised a specialist escalation; the operator later reported that the promised follow-up had not
arrived before the second incident.
The central defect is not that the Agent cannot explain itself. Replit operates the service,
meters the usage, issues the charge, and controls the records. The accountability
question belongs to Replit.
5. Repeated Prompt Generation Pattern
Operator-captured screenshots show multiple separately executed Workbook Engine prompts, each
marked approximately six days old, that repeat substantially the same audit instruction with minor
wording variation. The operator states that he did not submit these prompts.
The recurring pattern includes:
 Audit the current Workbook Engine state.
 Compare against the previous check, prior state, or last known baseline.
 Verify authorized full V1 scope and phase boundaries.

 Check for unauthorized scope reduction, skipped or weakened validation, regressions, product-family
integrity, Mixed Learning coverage, operator usability, cover quality, test/proof state,
deployment/recovery state, and operator-authority questions.
 Report only meaningful deltas.
 Do not modify, start, or authorize anything.
The screenshots show separate execution durations such as 37 seconds, 28 seconds, 23 seconds, 21

Repeated execution requires repeated provenance.

7. The Empty Visible Task-State Problem
In the contemporaneous support email reporting the second incident, the operator documented that the
Tasks interface showed Ready: 0, Active: 0, Draft: 0 while new execution activity was appearing.
Replit's public Task Board documentation describes Drafts as planned tasks not yet started, Active as tasks
currently building or queued behind the active-task limit, Ready as completed tasks awaiting review, and
Done as finished, archived, or cancelled work. The documentation says the board is intended to show
what Agent has planned, what is running, what is ready for review, and what has already been applied.
A user-facing task board that shows no Draft, Active, or Ready work does not prove that
no hidden backend mechanism exists. It does, however, leave the customer without a

[Source PDF page 9]
visible work item that accounts for later execution.
8. Business-Level Control Failure
Replit sells a product capable of autonomous or background execution that can directly create customer
charges. Where a customer disputes that execution, the company should be able to reconstruct the causal
and authority chain behind the billable event.
WHO / WHAT CREATED THE TASK
↓
WHAT PRIOR EVENT CAUSED IT
↓
WHAT AUTHORITY ALLOWED IT
↓
WHEN IT BECAME EXECUTABLE
↓
WHAT WORK IT PERFORMED
↓
WHAT USAGE IT CONSUMED
↓
WHAT CHARGE RESULTED
Without that chain, the customer is asked to trust the platform's downstream meter while the upstream
execution provenance remains unresolved.
The meter can prove consumption. It cannot, by itself, prove
authorization.
9. The Work-Order Test
Consider an ordinary repair transaction. A customer authorizes one repair. The shop later bills the
customer for additional work. The customer asks for the work order showing who approved the
additional repair and what work was performed. Showing that labor occurred or presenting an invoice
does not independently establish that the customer authorized the additional work.
The same evidentiary distinction applies here. Replit may be able to show that execution occurred, that
usage was metered, and that a charge resulted. When the customer disputes the execution, the separate
question is what authorized the run.
A company should not use its own system's execution as circular proof that the customer
authorized the execution.

10. Post-Notice Continuation
Before the second incident, Replit had already been placed on explicit notice that the operator disputed
unexpected Agent execution and associated charges. The operator specifically raised execution authority,
automatic restoration, queue release, background execution, spend authorization, billing attribution, and
missing task-level provenance.
The operator also supplied a concrete remediation model on September 18: separate billing eligibility,
project runtime, Agent execution authority, queue release, and spend authorization; after restoration,
keep Agent paused pending operator confirmation, hold queued work, and block new paid execution.
The second incident therefore occurred after both notice of the problem and a specific proposed control
response. This does not establish that Replit was legally required to implement the operator's architecture
immediately. It does establish that the control problem had been described to the company before the
later incident.
11. Cancellation Runway and Payment-Method Constraint
A September 26 operator screenshot shows the Replit Core subscription marked 'Cancels Oct 4' and states
that service will end on October 4, 2026. The same billing portal states that the default payment method
cannot be deleted because the account still has an active plan.

[Source PDF page 11]
Exhibit F - Cancellation state and payment-method retention shown in the billing portal.
Replit's cancellation structure appears to stop renewal while keeping service active until the end of the
paid term. That is not inherently improper. The control concern in this case is narrower: during a period
in which unexplained charge-generating execution was already disputed, the account remained
technically active and the default payment method remained attached.
Cancellation of renewal, continued service access, retained payment method, and
authority for new usage-based execution are separate states and should not be silently
collapsed.

12. Comparable Public Reports and Replit's Own Pricing Record
This incident is not presented as proof that Replit Agent systematically performs unauthorized work.
However, public material shows that unexpected autonomous/background activity, billing surprises,
looping behavior, and spend predictability have been recurring user concerns.
Replit's own July 12, 2025 Effort-Based Pricing Recap is especially relevant. Replit disclosed a July 11 cost-
calculation incident that affected approximately 6% of paying users and said all related charges would be
refunded or credited. The same post explains that newer Agent versions can run autonomously for
extended periods and that charges are tied to computing resources consumed.
Replit also acknowledged in that post that users had been surprised when Agent made code changes when
they intended only to chat or gain insight, and said it was working on features to distinguish dialogue
intent from intent to make changes.
Separately, Replit's Task Board documentation states that the board exists to help users understand
planned, active, ready, and completed Agent work. That makes task-state visibility directly relevant to
disputes involving unexplained execution.
Third-party reports should be treated as anecdotal context rather than proof of this account-specific
mechanism. Public Reddit discussions include allegations of runaway loops, surprise charges, weak
warning surfaces, and support frustration. These reports show that billing-control concerns are not
unique, but they do not establish that other users experienced the same initiating mechanism documented
here.
13. Evidence Boundaries
This case study does not claim that:
 Replit intentionally fabricated charges.
 Every Replit background execution is unauthorized.
 Every public user complaint reflects the same mechanism.
 The visible Task Board necessarily exposes every internal execution path.
 The technical root cause of the second incident has been conclusively established.
 Replit is legally liable; that question remains for the applicable dispute-resolution process.
The narrower supported finding is that multiple later audit-style executions appeared during a period
when the operator states he was not using Replit, the operator denies submitting the displayed prompts,
the visible task state did not identify Ready/Active/Draft work accounting for them, and Replit had already
said it lacked the granular task-level provenance requested for every run.
14. Required Resolution
For each disputed later execution, Replit should identify:
 run ID and parent task/job ID
 creation timestamp and execution-start timestamp
 creation source
 initiating actor or Replit system component
 trigger mechanism
 queue/task state
 prior authorization object or operator instruction

[Source PDF page 13]
 execution-authority state
 actions performed
 metered usage
 exact resulting charge
 invoice linkage
 whether the execution occurred after Replit received notice of the dispute
If no operator-authored parent task or authorization exists, Replit should identify the alternative system
mechanism that created the prompt/run and explain why that mechanism was permitted to create
customer-billable execution.
15. Requested Remediation
 Preserve all execution, Agent, deployment, queue, account-state, and billing logs relevant to Workbook
Engine and the disputed period.
 Reconstruct the complete provenance chain for the second incident and each repeated prompt/run.
 Reconcile every disputed run with the exact resulting charge.
 Refund all charges Replit cannot tie to a clearly attributable operator authorization event.
 Separate billing eligibility, service availability, Agent execution authority, queue release authority, and
spend authorization in product controls.
 Require explicit post-restoration confirmation before previously held or newly generated paid Agent
work can execute.
 Expose all charge-capable background, deployment, retry, scheduled, and Agent-created execution
paths in a user-visible control surface.
 Provide durable run-level provenance sufficient to audit who/what initiated each billable execution.
16. Governing Finding
The first incident raised a restoration question: can payment or billing restoration automatically release
previously authorized work?
The second incident raises a more fundamental attribution question: what happens when new prompts
and executions appear after the earlier work is gone, the customer states that he did not submit those
prompts, and the service provider cannot produce a complete task-level provenance chain?
The service provider owns the execution system. The service
provider issues the charge. The service provider should be able to
establish the chain connecting the two to the customer's authority.
If Replit cannot identify who or what created a disputed billable
run, the existence of metered usage should not be treated as
sufficient proof that the customer authorized it.
