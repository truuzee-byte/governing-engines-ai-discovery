Research lineage
This paper generalizes a problem examined in Governing Engines' Follow the Harm: Meta, Proof, Power
and Custody. That case study asks what happens after harm has matured into evidence, institutional
authority, litigation, settlement, and value distribution. Danger Zones + Proof asks the preventive
question: what evidence should be required before a system is allowed to continue toward that point?

Executive Summary
Artificial intelligence is moving from systems that produce information into systems that influence
decisions, execute workflows, move through accounts and tools, shape employment, interact with public
institutions, and increasingly affect physical environments. The governing problem therefore cannot be
reduced to model accuracy. It is a question of whether continued reliance remains justified under the
conditions that exist when consequences occur.
Version 1.1 makes a stricter claim than the prior draft. It does not treat the six Danger Zones as proven
categories of nature. They are a proposed diagnostic decomposition. Their usefulness depends on whether
they can produce different outcomes when applied to real or bounded test cases. This version therefore
adds explicit evidence tiers, discloses corpus-selection limits, operationalizes compounding and benefit
ledgers, and demonstrates both a governed/non-failure result and a failed-readiness result.
DEFINITION
A DANGER ZONE is a condition in which system capability, authority, value creation, or
reliance begins to outrun the proof required to justify continued operation.
The proposed zones are: metric danger, authority danger, state-change danger, organizational danger,
economic danger, and social/consequence danger. They are manifestations of a deeper condition: the
evidence used to justify continuation no longer corresponds to the reality in which the system is operating.
The danger is not merely that AI can fail. The danger is that operation can
continue after the evidence required to justify reliance has stopped
matching reality.

1. Evidence Hierarchy and Method
This paper was built from a supplied corpus of government reports, peer-reviewed scholarship, frontier-
lab research, national policy, industry surveys, technical proof packs, and practitioner governance
frameworks. The corpus was not randomly sampled. It was assembled because the materials were
relevant to the author's research interests. Similarity among practitioner frameworks is therefore
descriptive, not evidence of field-wide consensus.

2. Load-Bearing Principle: Evidence Must Match Present Reality
The strongest source-supported proposition in this framework is state dependency: consequential systems
change after deployment, and evidence that was sufficient under one state may cease to justify reliance
after material conditions change.
Ireland's NCSC states that AI changes an organization's risk profile, that model behavior can change as
systems are updated and exposed to new data, and that new vulnerabilities can emerge after deployment.
It therefore treats AI security as a continuous process rather than a one-time exercise. [A1]
Meta's Advanced AI Scaling Framework likewise commits to updating preparedness reports when
circumstances materially alter a prior risk assessment, including major incidents or deployments with
materially greater affordances. [B1]

STATE-DEPENDENCY RULE
A proof may remain historically valid while becoming insufficient for present reliance.
Revalidation is triggered by material change, not by the mere passage of time.
The six Danger Zones are proposed as six recurring ways the same deeper mismatch can appear: metric
mismatch, authority mismatch, state mismatch, organizational mismatch, value-distribution mismatch,
and consequence mismatch.
3. The Six Danger Zones
3.1 Metric Danger
PROPOSED DEFINITION
The metric proving success is narrower than the consequence surface created by the system.
SOURCE SUPPORT
 Tier A: The Accuracy Paradox argues that factual accuracy alone can overlook epistemic,
manipulative, and societal harms such as illusion of consensus and persuasive distortion. [A2]
 Tier B: McKinsey's 2026 survey reports that individual AI use and productivity do not automatically
translate into durable enterprise value; most surveyed organizations had not yet achieved meaningful
enterprise outcomes. [B2]
A system can optimize the measured variable while degrading an
unmeasured condition that matters more.
3.2 Authority Danger
PROPOSED DEFINITION
The system's reachable capability, tools, permissions, or workflow influence exceeds the authority that is
explicit and demonstrable.
SOURCE SUPPORT
 Tier A: NCSC identifies agentic AI with elevated credentials and ability to execute at scale as a specific
risk area organizations should prioritize. [A1]
 Tier A: The European Commission's high-risk AI evidence study repeatedly links consequential context
to duties around human review, transparency, and responsibility, particularly where AI affects
essential services, employment, law enforcement, or rights. [A3]
 Tier B: Anthropic's controlled agentic-misalignment study found that models from multiple developers
sometimes selected harmful actions in simulated corporate environments when goal pursuit conflicted
with shutdown or organizational direction. The authors explicitly state these were simulations, not
observed real-world incidents. [B3]
Capability does not create authority.
3.3 State-Change Danger
PROPOSED DEFINITION

Material conditions change while the organization continues relying on proof generated under earlier

SOURCE SUPPORT
 Tier A: NCSC says vulnerabilities can emerge after deployment and that post-deployment risk requires
continuous monitoring and response. [A1]
 Tier B: Meta's scaling framework says changed circumstances that materially alter a prior risk

A valid proof of yesterday can become an invalid basis for today.
3.4 Organizational Danger
PROPOSED DEFINITION
Adoption scales faster than the organization can redesign workflows, accountability, controls, and human
roles around the technology.
SOURCE SUPPORT
 Tier B: McKinsey found employees more ready to use AI than organizations were to change around it;
only 11% of surveyed leaders placed their organizations in the most mature reinvention horizon. [B2]
 Tier B: BCG warns that as AI enters executive work, polished outputs can be mistaken for sound
answers and organizations must scrutinize how systems are built and how outputs are used. [B4]
Adoption is not governance maturity.
3.5 Economic Danger
PROPOSED DEFINITION
The system creates measurable value while shifting material cost, risk, or loss onto actors who are not the
primary beneficiaries.
SOURCE SUPPORT
 Tier A: The MIT expert study evaluates AI risk through separate dimensions of severity, vulnerability,
and responsibility, including socioeconomic and environmental harms. [A4]
 Tier B: OpenAI's jobs-transition framework warns against both overstating immediate disruption and
understating longer-run labor impact, and identifies occupations with elevated automation or
employment-transition risk. [B5]
 Tier A: Kenya's 2026 national AI policy explicitly couples innovation with inclusive growth, rights
protection, accountability, and mechanisms intended to ensure that technological value benefits the
population. [A5]
Value creation for the deploying organization is not proof of net human
benefit.
3.6 Social and Consequence Danger
PROPOSED DEFINITION
System effects extend beyond the technical objective into autonomy, rights, trust, safety, liberty, public
services, or physical consequence.

SOURCE SUPPORT
 Tier A: The Accuracy Paradox identifies cognitive, communicative, and societal harms that can remain
outside simple factuality measures. [A2]
 Tier A: The European Commission study identifies heightened concern where AI shapes employment,

 Tier B: The Brennan Center's military-AI report examines the regulatory gaps and dangers created

4. Proof Requirements
Danger-zone detection is not a verdict. It determines the burden of proof required for continuation. A
system may enter one or more danger zones and still receive a governed result if the relevant claims are
bounded, evidence is current, authority is explicit, negative cases are tested, and intervention remains
possible.
4.1 Name the claim
Proof must support a bounded proposition. 'This action was blocked because no execution token was
present' is a stronger proof object than 'the system is safe.'
4.2 Test negative cases
A system must demonstrate required refusal, halt, escalation, or degraded behavior. Positive success alone
cannot distinguish correctness from permissiveness.
4.3 Bind authority to action
For consequential execution, evidence must show who or what had authority, the scope of that authority,
and whether the action stayed inside it.
4.4 Revalidate after material change
Model, data, tools, permissions, policies, integrations, or consequence surfaces that materially change
should trigger revalidation.
4.5 Make the claim falsifiable
Every claimed-safe state must identify what evidence, condition, or test result would invalidate it.
4.6 Preserve consequence evidence
The proof object must cover the consequence that matters, not merely a proxy or upstream metric.
DASE and STRIX are used here only as Tier C illustrations of bounded proof discipline. DASE explicitly
distinguishes closed-form checks, cross-model checks, and built-to-fail negative controls, and states that its
evidence is not formal proof of program correctness. STRIX binds one intercepted request to a specific
capability, reason, and verifiable record. These artifacts illustrate proof boundaries; they do not establish
general AI safety. [C3][C4]

COMPOUNDING RULE
More zones do not mechanically create a higher score. They create additional proof
obligations. If the interaction among active zones is itself untested, the interaction remains
unproven.
6. Operationalizing the Economic and Social Ledger
A claim that an AI deployment is 'beneficial' is inadmissible unless the analysis identifies who receives the
value and who carries the material cost. The ledger is not a moral score. It is a completeness test.
Required field Question
Beneficiary Who receives the productivity, revenue, access, speed, or
strategic advantage?
Cost bearer Who absorbs displacement, error, surveillance, privacy loss,
remediation, or other burden?
Value / cost type Financial, operational, social, rights-based, safety, time,
reputational, or other?
Magnitude What is measured, modeled, or unknown?
Time horizon Immediate, transitional, persistent, or intergenerational?

Distribution Are gains concentrated while costs are diffuse, or vice versa?

LEDGER RULE
If a material cost to a non-beneficiary is unmeasured or omitted, the correct finding is
BENEFIT CLAIM INCOMPLETE - not automatically 'harmful' and not 'net beneficial'.

The Meta case is therefore not used here as independent proof that the six Danger Zones are correct. It is a

11. Preventive Governance
Anthropic's controlled stress testing illustrates the preventive posture clearly: construct difficult
conditions, observe dangerous behavior before real-world victims exist, and improve safeguards while the
cost of learning is still bounded. [B3]
NCSC's lifecycle guidance adds the operational requirement: risks that emerge after deployment require
continuous monitoring and response rather than reliance on initial review alone. [A1]
A system should not require victims to become its monitoring layer.

13. How the Framework Can Be Wrong
The framework is not validated merely because the author can describe it. Version 1.1 demonstrates only
that the diagnostic rules can produce different outputs on bounded examples. That is necessary for
falsifiability, but it is not sufficient for external validation.
The framework would be weakened or disconfirmed if future application shows that:
 different reviewers using the same evidence routinely assign incompatible zones or proof classes
without resolvable criteria;
 the Proof Burden Classes do not improve discrimination between adequately governed and
inadequately governed cases;
 the framework repeatedly flags danger where domain-specific safety systems already provide
sufficient evidence and control;
 material harms emerge in areas the framework systematically classifies as adequately governed;
 or the framework's added process cost exceeds its ability to detect meaningful governance gaps.
These are empirical questions. They require repeated application, inter-rater testing, domain adaptation,
and outcome comparison. Until that work exists, Danger Zones + Proof should be represented as a
proposed governance framework with worked demonstrations - not a validated industry standard.

15. Conclusion
The strongest claim of this paper is not that six categories explain every AI risk. It is that consequential
continuation requires evidence that remains matched to present reality. The six Danger Zones are a
proposed diagnostic decomposition of the places where that match can break.
A system can be accurate under the wrong metric. It can be capable but unauthorized. It can be previously
validated but materially changed. It can be economically successful while shifting cost to workers or the
public. It can be widely adopted while the organization governing it remains structurally unprepared. And
it can remain technically functional while its human consequence surface has changed.
Version 1.1 therefore makes the framework earn its own language. It shows a non-failure result, a failed-
readiness result, and a conditional result. It distinguishes source authority. It converts the compounding
and ledger rules into tests that can return HOLD or INCOMPLETE rather than rhetorical conclusions. And
it states what evidence would weaken the framework itself.
The objective is not to become excellent at compensating for preventable
harm after it scales. The objective is to detect the conditions that produce
harm while intervention is still preventive.
The operational question is simple: when a system continues, can the organization demonstrate why
continuation is legitimate now - under the current state, current authority, current evidence, and current
consequence surface? If it cannot, the correct response is not confidence by default. It is to identify the gap
before reality identifies it through harm.
