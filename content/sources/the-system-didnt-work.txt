Abstract
Modern systems are frequently evaluated through proxies. A completed task can stand in for reliable
execution. A credential can stand in for competence. Adoption can stand in for value. Institutional
pedigree can stand in for capability. Recognition can stand in for support. A governance policy can stand
in for governed execution. Historical evidence can stand in for present validity.
These substitutions are often useful. Complex institutions cannot directly reevaluate every underlying
property continuously. Problems emerge when a proxy is allowed to answer a question it cannot actually
resolve, or when direct contradictory evidence is ignored.
This paper examines that failure across technology, artificial intelligence, employment, credentialing,
startup finance, institutional access, organizational governance, and human-machine interaction. It
develops six related concepts: Reliability Theater, Proof-Class Confusion, Capability-Legibility Burden,
Proof Saturation, Recognition Without Allocation, and Hidden Builder Burden. The unifying mechanism
proposed is Proxy Substitution.
The paper does not argue that credentials, reputation, governance policies, screening criteria, investment
diligence, or human oversight are illegitimate. It argues for evidence-question alignment: evidence should
be judged against the question it is capable of answering. When an underlying capability can be
examined directly, claimant proxies should not displace relevant evidence about that capability. When
operators must compensate for system failure, that compensation should be visible in the performance
record.
The governing question is not merely: Did it work? It is: What actually
made it work?
Author's Note: Why This Question Was Pursued
This research did not begin as an academic exercise. It began with a recurring personal observation:
many people could identify conditions they believed should change, yet action repeatedly stopped at the
boundaries of existing systems.
What began as frustration became a series of questions about capability, authority, proof, participation,
institutional design, and the difference between what systems claim to value and what they actually
reward. Encountering generative AI accelerated the inquiry by making it possible to interrogate questions,
compare domains, formalize intuitions, and test whether privately held observations could survive
articulation and external evidence.
The author's experience generated those questions. It does not serve as evidence for their generality.
The purpose of the research that follows is to determine how far independent evidence allows those
questions to travel.
The personal case explains why the inquiry began. The evidence must
explain what the inquiry found.
Scope
This paper is not a theory of all institutional decision-making. It studies a narrower family of evaluation
failures: cases in which proxy evidence, hidden compensation, undefined proof requirements, or
institutional legibility distort evaluation of an underlying property.

PART I - THE OPERATOR BEHIND THE SUCCESS
1. Outcome Is Not Mechanism
Modern systems are heavily outcome-oriented. Did the workflow complete? Did the customer receive the
result? Did the model answer? Did production stay online? Did the employee finish the task? Those
questions are legitimate. They become misleading when the outcome is recorded while the mechanism
required to achieve it disappears.
Imagine a car whose brakes have failed. The driver discovers that throwing an anchor from the window at
the right moment will stop the vehicle. The maneuver works. Trips continue. Crashes are avoided. The
transportation record may show successful arrival. But the braking system did not become reliable. The
driver became skilled at compensating for its failure.
The analogy concerns attribution, not equivalence between mechanical and software failure.
Automation research has recognized versions of this problem for decades. Bainbridge (1983) observed
that automation can expand rather than eliminate human-operator problems, particularly when humans
remain responsible for abnormal conditions and recovery. Later work on the out-of-the-loop performance
problem describes how highly automated environments can reduce operator understanding, vigilance,
and takeover performance when automation behaves unexpectedly (Gouraud, Delorme, & Berberian,
2017).
ESTABLISHED  Automation can shift abnormal-condition and recovery burdens to human operators,
and high automation can create out-of-the-loop performance problems.
2. The Anchor Problem and Operator Rescue
Human adaptation is not itself evidence of system failure. Complex systems often depend on human
flexibility. Some human-machine systems are intentionally collaborative. The relevant distinction is
between designed human contribution and unrecorded human rescue.
DEFINITION  Operator rescue occurs when successful completion requires compensating action that
is not part of the system's intended or represented operating procedure and that addresses a system
deficiency, contradiction, or failure state.
Entering a password is normal operation. Reviewing a high-risk decision because the system is explicitly
designed for human review is normal operation. Retrying a workflow because the system falsely declared
success, reconciling contradictory states because no authoritative state exists, or manually repairing
broken state in a nominally autonomous process are different.
A workaround is not reliability. It is fault tolerance performed by the
user.
3. Reliability Theater
This paper proposes the term Reliability Theater for situations in which a system receives credit for
successful outcomes that materially depend on hidden operator compensation.
system fails -> operator intervenes -> outcome survives -> system
receives the success

Workaround research supports the underlying phenomenon. Davison, Wong, Ou, and Alter (2021)
studied employees using a mandated enterprise system that did not fit local realities. Employees devised
coordinated workarounds that violated formal IT policy but enabled essential activities to continue. Wong
et al. (2022), using data from 310 enterprise-system users, similarly found that inadequate information
systems can induce workaround behavior.
ESTABLISHED  Operators and employees create compensating workarounds when formal
information systems inadequately support real work.
The existence of a workaround does not automatically prove bad system design. Local adaptation can be
valuable. The accounting problem begins when the workaround materially supports the outcome but
disappears from the performance claim.
A bounded Governing Engines case, Connected Is Not Operational, documents a declared GitHub
integration state that exposed authorization and capabilities while runtime retrieval could not reach a
known repository. The case is used only to illustrate the narrower distinction between declared capability
and observed execution (Governing Engines, 2026b).
If the operator had to rescue the outcome, the system did not succeed
alone.
4. Hidden Labor and Performance Accounting
Consider two systems that both report a 95 percent task-completion rate. System A completes those
tasks without unplanned intervention. System B reaches the same rate only after retries, manual
corrections, state resets, expert escalation, and data cleanup. A completion metric treats them as
equivalent. Operational reality does not.
The evidence currently establishes that workarounds and recovery burdens exist. It does not establish
that every organization systematically omits them from its metrics. This paper therefore treats the claim
that successful outcomes can conceal operator labor as SUPPORTED, and the accounting rule below as
PROPOSED.
SUPPORTED  Successful outcomes can conceal material operator intervention and recovery effort.
PROPOSED GOVERNING RULE  If the operator must compensate for a system failure, that
compensation belongs in the system's performance record.
A more complete performance record could distinguish nominal completion from recovery-dependent
completion. Where appropriate, evaluation can include unplanned-intervention rate, retry count, manual
correction rate, override rate, escalation frequency, recovery time, expert intervention, workaround
dependence, false-success rate, state-reconciliation burden, and operator sensitivity.

4.1. Mechanism-Cost Separation
Outcome similarity can conceal radically different production mechanisms. Two systems may produce the
same visible result while differing in computation, energy, labor, intervention, latency, infrastructure, or
recovery burden. The paper therefore extends Outcome Is Not Mechanism into a second accounting rule:
equivalent-looking outputs do not establish equivalent systems.
PROPOSED
TERM
Mechanism-Cost Separation: the distinction between what an output looks like and the
mechanism, resources, intervention, and cost required to produce it.
• Output equivalence is not mechanism equivalence. Similar answers, task completions, or service outcomes
may arise from very different architectures.
• Mechanism matters when evaluating efficiency. A system can appear efficient at the output layer while
externalizing energy, operator labor, infrastructure, or recovery cost.
• Resource accounting belongs with performance accounting. When a comparison claims one method is
superior, the comparison should state which resource boundary is being measured.
• Human-machine comparisons require property discipline. Capability, energy efficiency, speed, cost,
adaptability, and robustness are distinct properties; one should not substitute for another.
Equivalent-looking outputs do not imply equivalent mechanisms,
resource costs, or system efficiency.
This section does not adopt unverified social-media energy comparisons as empirical evidence. It preserves the narrower
analytical distinction those comparisons illustrate.

PART II - PROOF IS NOT ONE THING
5. The Proof Bucket Problem
Institutions often use the word proof as though it were one evidentiary category. It is not. A working
demo, diploma, license, benchmark, customer contract, safety certification, system log, prior exit, and
founder resume can all be described as proof, but they answer different questions.
More evidence is not necessarily better evidence if it answers the
wrong question.
6. Evidence Classes
Evidence class Question answered
Capability evidence Can the thing perform the claimed function?
Reliability evidence Can it perform consistently under relevant
conditions?
Operational evidence Does it work in the environment where it must
actually operate?
Safety and risk evidence What happens when it fails, and are
consequences acceptably controlled?
Authority evidence Does the actor possess the legal, professional,
organizational, or delegated authority required to
act?
Ownership and provenance evidence Who created, owns, controls, or is authorized to
use the asset?
Transaction evidence Can the parties legally and practically complete
and perform the proposed transaction?
Market evidence Is there demonstrated demand, adoption,
purchasing behavior, or willingness to pay?
Scale evidence Does acceptable behavior persist as workload,
users, integrations, or organizational complexity
increase?
Institutional legibility signals Does the person or organization display signals
the institution recognizes as credibility or
readiness indicators?
Institutional legibility is intentionally described as a signal rather than a proof class. It may be useful for
screening, but familiarity with an institutionally recognized signal does not itself establish the underlying
technical property.
7. Evidence-Question Alignment
Evidence should be judged against the question it is capable of
answering.

If the question is whether software performs a function, direct observation of the software performing that
function is highly relevant. If the question is whether the claimant owns the software, provenance and
legal documentation become relevant. If the question is safety, risk evidence is required. If the question is
whether the organization can fulfill a contract, transaction and continuity evidence matter.
Evidence weight should reflect proximity to the property, validity of the test, representativeness, and
relevance to the decision. Direct evidence is therefore not automatically conclusive: a one-time
demonstration may establish capability while providing weak reliability evidence.
8. Screening Is Not Adjudication
Institutions often need inexpensive first-stage screens. A resume, degree, company age, or
organizational form may be rational as a screening device when thousands of cases cannot all receive
expensive substantive review.
This paper distinguishes screening evidence from adjudicative evidence. Screening evidence helps
decide what receives deeper evaluation. Adjudicative evidence determines whether the underlying
proposition is actually supported.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE  A screening proxy becomes problematic when it permanently substitutes for
substantive evaluation of an independently testable property.
9. The Talking Horse Test
Suppose someone says: "I have a horse that can talk." The capability claim is extraordinary. The most
direct question is obvious: does the horse talk? The claimant's education, family background, investor
endorsements, accelerator membership, and resume do not answer that capability question.
Those facts may matter to different questions: does the claimant own the horse, can it be legally sold, is
delivery credible, or can the behavior be reproduced consistently? But they remain separate evidence
classes.
The resume does not make the horse talk.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE  Evaluate the thing first where the thing can be evaluated directly. Evaluate
the claimant separately where claimant properties actually matter.
The standard applies in both directions. Elite pedigree should not reduce the evidence required for a
capability claim. Lack of pedigree should not increase it merely because the expected social signal is
absent.

PART III - CAPABILITY, LEGIBILITY, AND ACCESS
10. System Properties Are Not Founder Properties
Institutions repeatedly say they care about system properties such as reliability, robustness, accuracy,
security, privacy, traceability, auditability, controllability, recoverability, scalability, operational fit, safety,
and economic value. UNESCO's 2026 AI governance survey describes standards and high-risk
requirements involving risk management, data quality, documentation, event logging, transparency,
human oversight, accuracy, robustness, and cybersecurity (UNESCO, 2026).
Those are system and deployment properties. A prestigious school does not establish robustness. A
famous investor does not establish auditability. A polished company biography does not establish
reliability.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE  When an institution invokes a particular system property as the basis of
evaluation, evidence unrelated to that property should not be treated as proof of the property itself.
This does not mean investors or accelerators must evaluate only technology. They may rationally
evaluate the entire company, including team, legal structure, execution ability, market, and continuity.
The point is semantic and evidentiary: capability proof does not automatically create transaction trust,
and claimant signals do not themselves establish system capability.
11. Capability vs. Legibility
Capability asks whether a person or system can actually perform. Legibility asks whether an institution
can recognize, categorize, compare, and trust that capability using signals it already understands.
Capability and legibility often correlate. They are not identical. Someone can be highly legible without
being exceptional. Someone can be highly capable without being institutionally legible.
The expensive part is not always becoming capable. Sometimes it is
becoming legible to people who control resources.
Legibility can require credentials, corporate structure, references, institutional endorsements, revenue,
professional branding, named executives, market traction, accelerator participation, or social proximity to
trusted networks. These signals can reduce transaction cost and uncertainty. The failure occurs when
legibility substitutes for examination of capability itself.
12. Capability-Legibility Burden
This paper proposes Capability-Legibility Burden as the cost imposed when capable actors must
repeatedly translate demonstrated ability into institutional forms before resource holders will act on it.
capability exists -> work demonstrates capability -> capability remains
difficult to classify -> additional institutional signals are produced ->
translation cost continues
The translation cost may include documentation, business formation, websites, pitch materials,
certifications, testing, branding, applications, traction development, network-building, additional hires, and
uncompensated development. Some of this may strengthen the venture. Some may primarily increase
institutional readability rather than underlying capability.

13. Institutional Access Gating: A Bounded Case
An August 2026 exchange with NVIDIA's Inception startup program provides a bounded illustration. The
program communicated eligibility conditions including a functional company website, company-age
restrictions, and two distinct named contacts covering development and business leadership before
evaluation through the program's application pathway. The underlying correspondence is retained by the
author.
The evidence does not establish that NVIDIA rejected the underlying technology, considered it technically
weak, or would have accepted it absent those criteria. Large programs may rationally use organizational
readiness requirements for serviceability, fraud prevention, capacity, or workflow reasons.
OBSERVED CASE  Access to a technology-support pathway can depend on organizational eligibility
criteria before the underlying technical capability is substantively evaluated.
The relevant distinction is not fairness versus unfairness. It is property separation: organizational
readiness and technical capability are different questions.

PART IV - CREDENTIALS, AUTHORITY, AND COMPETENCE
14. What a Credential Can Establish
Credentials can carry real institutional value. They may establish recognized education, supervised
practice, legal authorization, continuing education status, minimum competency thresholds,
accountability, or permission to act in regulated domains.
A current credential is therefore not merely historical. In some fields it establishes present authority. But
authority and operational competence remain distinct properties.
Credentials can establish authority. Competence remains an
operational property.
Ericsson (2008) notes that traditional expertise indicators such as length of experience, reputation, and
perceived mastery have only weak relationships with directly observed performance in the literature he
reviews. The correct inference is not that credentials or experience are useless. It is that indirect
qualification indicators should not be treated as equivalent to observed present performance.
15. Credential Gating and Degree Preference
Credential gating occurs when access to an opportunity is materially determined by an accepted
institutional signal before relevant capability is substantively evaluated. Some gates are legally necessary
or operationally rational. Others function primarily as screening proxies.
Research from Harvard Business School's Project on Managing the Future of Work and the Burning
Glass Institute found that many employers announced removal of degree requirements while sustained
changes in actual hiring remained limited. Their 2024 report summarized the aggregate opportunity
expansion as not even one in 700 hires (Fuller et al., 2024).
ESTABLISHED  Removing a formal degree requirement does not necessarily produce a
corresponding change in actual hiring outcomes.
Removing a credential requirement is not the same as removing
credential preference.
16. The Recursive Credential Trap
This paper proposes a cumulative mechanism. A recognized credential can provide access to salary,
equipment, mentors, supervised practice, references, recognized experience, and further opportunity.
Those conditions can then strengthen future capability and future institutional signals.
credential -> access -> recognized experience -> stronger signal ->
greater future access
The inverse can also occur: lack of an accepted credential reduces access, which reduces recognized
experience, which weakens future signals. This mechanism is presented as PROPOSED/SUPPORTED
rather than universal. It is consistent with broader research traditions on cumulative advantage and
signaling, but should be tested by occupation and institution.

17. Skill Formation Is a Pipeline-Design Question
A positive counterexample appears in school-based trade pathways that combine early practical training,
recognized qualification, and work access. The Texas plumbing case discussed during this research
illustrates how training, examination, and workforce entry can be sequenced closer together rather than
delayed behind a four-year degree pathway.
PROPOSED CLAIM  Some apparent skill shortages may partly reflect pathway design rather than an
absolute absence of people capable of acquiring the skill.
This claim is intentionally bounded. It requires occupational and jurisdictional testing and should not be
generalized across professions with materially different safety, legal, or educational requirements.

PART V - CAPITAL AND THE PROOF CONTRADICTION
18. Proof Requires Resources
Technology development is an uncertainty-reduction process. Reliability is produced through
engineering, testing, failure, iteration, infrastructure, hiring, and time. Those activities require resources.
The U.S. Government Accountability Office has documented a commercialization "valley of death" in
which promising technologies lack the funding needed for further development and demonstration while
private partners remain reluctant to invest until prototypes, performance data, or similar evidence exists.
In GAO-09-548, technology-transfer officials at 14 of 17 Department of Energy laboratories identified lack
of funding for additional development or testing as a significant constraint (U.S. GAO, 2009).
ESTABLISHED  A funding-proof recursion exists in technology commercialization: additional evidence
can require resources that are themselves difficult to obtain before the evidence exists.
PROPOSED TERM  The Proof Contradiction: some systems receive resources to become reliable;
others are asked to become reliable before receiving resources.
The concept does not imply that every unfinished project deserves financing. It identifies the
developmental importance of when resources enter.
19. Formation-Stage Capital and Proof-Stage Capital
This paper proposes the terms formation-stage capital and proof-stage capital as analytical categories,
not standard industry classifications.
Formation-stage capital enters while substantial uncertainty remains and finances the process of
generating stronger evidence: engineering, prototyping, testing, infrastructure, hiring, market discovery,
and iteration.
Proof-stage capital enters after substantial technical, operational, or market uncertainty has already been
reduced.
Capital does not merely reward proof. Capital can finance the
production of proof.
Neither category is inherently superior. They create different developmental environments and different
distributions of uncertainty-bearing.
20. Capital Allocation Contains Social Variables
Capital markets are not engineering benchmarks. Investors evaluate expected return, team, market,
timing, information, risk, network signals, prior history, and many other variables.
Abakah, Kim, and Somé (2024) found that startups in U.S. counties with greater social proximity to
startup funding attracted larger investor bases and secured more first-round capital. The finding is
associational and should not be read as proof that connections determine every funding outcome.
SUPPORTED  Access to startup capital is influenced by social and institutional position in addition to
characteristics of the underlying venture.

21. Capability Does Not Create Entitlement to Allocation
A central boundary of this paper is explicit: demonstrated capability creates evidence of capability. It does
not create an entitlement to capital, procurement, employment, or institutional participation.
An investor may decline because of mandate, timing, market size, portfolio construction, legal risk, team
risk, or expected return. An accelerator may impose readiness criteria. An employer may need authority
or continuity evidence beyond capability.
The demand is not: fund every capable thing. The demand is: name the
property actually blocking the decision.

PART V - CAPITAL AND THE PROOF CONTRADICTION
21.1. Infrastructure-Created Markets
Formation-stage capital does more than finance a venture inside an already visible market. In some domains,
infrastructure is part of what makes the later market possible at all. Transportation, communications,
compute, energy, standards, logistics, and production capacity can reduce constraints, lower transaction
cost, expose new use cases, and create conditions under which previously uneconomic activity becomes
viable.
PROPOSED
TERM
Infrastructure-Created Market: an economic opportunity whose practical demand, scale,
or transaction viability depends materially on infrastructure that must be financed before
the market is fully observable.
• Demand can be endogenous to infrastructure. What looks like weak demand before enabling infrastructure
may become a different market after access, cost, reliability, or scale changes.
• Formation-stage judgment therefore evaluates possibility under uncertainty. It asks whether financing the
enabling conditions could produce a viable future system, not merely whether a mature market already
exists.
• Proof-stage judgment evaluates a different state. Once the infrastructure exists and demand becomes
legible, uncertainty has already been reduced by earlier capital and execution.
• Retrospective success should preserve sequence. Later market size should not erase who financed the
uncertain infrastructure that made the market measurable.
If you only recognize the market after the infrastructure makes it
obvious, you are measuring certainty - not judgment.
PROPOSED ANALYTICAL EXTENSION. The concept does not imply that infrastructure spending guarantees future demand. It
identifies a class of allocation decisions in which the financed object partly creates the conditions later used as proof.

PART VI - PROOF SATURATION AND DECISION-REASON
INTEGRITY
22. Proof Must Resolve a Defined Uncertainty
Proof only has meaning relative to a proposition. A useful evidence request should identify what remains
uncertain, what evidence could resolve it, what result would count as sufficient, and what result would
weaken or disconfirm the claim.
Prior Governing Engines research applies the same bounded-proof discipline: historical proof can remain
valid as a record of a prior state while becoming insufficient for current reliance after material change
(Governing Engines, 2026a).
23. Proof Saturation
This paper proposes Proof Saturation as a diagnostic condition, not a founder's subjective declaration
that enough evidence has been provided.
Proof Saturation is present when all of the following conditions are substantially met:
1. The claim under evaluation is explicitly defined.
2. Relevant evidence responsive to that claim has been supplied.
3. Previously stated thresholds have been satisfied or materially addressed.
4. Additional evidence continues to be requested.
5. The remaining uncertainty is not clearly specified.
6. No decision rule identifies what evidence would materially change the outcome.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE  When proof keeps increasing but the decision does not change, the
bottleneck may no longer be proof.
24. Moving Proof Standards
Changing proof requirements can be legitimate. New authority, broader deployment, higher
consequence, new integrations, or changed state can justify stronger evidence. The problem is not
movement. The problem is ungoverned movement.
A changed standard should identify the new uncertainty, explain why prior evidence is insufficient for the
present decision, and state what new evidence could alter the decision.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE  A proof standard stops functioning as validation when satisfying it cannot
materially change the decision.
25. Decision-Reason Integrity
A separate failure occurs when the stated reason for a decision does not correspond to the condition that
could actually change the decision. An evaluator may rationally decline for mandate, budget, timing,
team, or portfolio reasons. If the stated reason is instead "more proof" when no amount of the requested
proof could alter the outcome, the builder is sent into an economically wasteful loop.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE  The stated reason for a decision should correspond to the condition that
could actually change that decision.

PART VII - RECOGNITION, ALLOCATION, AND THE HIDDEN
BUILDER BURDEN
26. Recognition Without Allocation
Recognition is not meaningless. It indicates attention or perceived value. But recognition is informational;
allocation is consequential.
Views, follows, comments, profile visits, introductions, praise, and "keep building" messages can
demonstrate attention. They do not automatically demonstrate purchasing behavior, capital commitment,
distribution, labor contribution, procurement, or institutional sponsorship.
Recognition is not allocation.
For precision, this paper avoids treating casual praise as validation. Even repeated positive validation,
however, remains distinct from allocation.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE  An idea can be recognized repeatedly and supported never.
27. Allocation Accounting Error
This paper replaces the broader term Market Accounting Error with Allocation Accounting Error.
ALLOCATION ACCOUNTING ERROR  Observed reality: recognition occurs. Recorded interpretation:
meaningful support exists. Missing variable: committed resource.
The concept applies beyond markets - to professional networks, research ecosystems, public programs,
startup communities, and institutions.

PART XI - THE UNIFYING MECHANISM: PROXY SUBSTITUTION
35. Why Proxies Exist
Proxies are not inherently defective. Institutions use them because direct evaluation can be expensive,
uncertain, slow, or impossible to perform continuously. A proxy need not be identical to the underlying
property to be useful. It may retain sufficient predictive or administrative value for its intended purpose.
The failure begins when a proxy is treated as conclusive evidence outside that purpose, when one
evidence class is allowed to answer another question, or when contradictory direct evidence is ignored.
36. Proxy Substitution
The recurring substitutions examined in this paper include:
 workaround -> reliability
 credential -> competence
 resume -> capability
 organizational form -> readiness
 usage -> value
 recognition -> support
 historical evidence -> present validity
 policy -> governance
 authorization -> operability
 successful output -> successful system
The error is not using a proxy. The error is forgetting what the proxy
cannot prove.
37. A Better Evaluation Architecture
A better evaluation process begins by naming the property under review, selecting evidence capable of
answering that question, distinguishing screening from adjudication, and recording the actual reason for
the decision.
1. Name the property: capability, reliability, authority, safety, market demand, organizational readiness,
or another defined question.
2. Select relevant evidence and state what the evidence can and cannot establish.
3. Use screening proxies for triage where necessary, but do not silently convert them into final
adjudication.
4. When direct evidence exists, weight it by validity, representativeness, and proximity to the property
rather than by prestige alone.
5. If the decision is negative, state the actual blocker and whether additional evidence could change the
outcome.
6. If human recovery materially supports performance, include that intervention in the performance
record.

PART XIII - JUDGMENT AND INSTITUTIONAL LEGITIMACY
40. Judgment as a Continuing Legitimacy Property
Institutions often possess formal authority to make consequential decisions: universities certify, investors allocate, regulators
authorize, employers hire, boards govern, and public bodies adjudicate. Authority answers whether an institution may decide. It
does not, by itself, answer whether the institution is deciding well.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE
An institution does not remain legitimate merely because it has authority to judge. Continued legitimacy
also depends on whether its judgment remains worthy of reliance.
For this framework, institutional judgment is the ability to make decisions that are evidence-aligned, proportionate to uncertainty,
responsive to reality, and correctable when new evidence appears. It does not require perfect foresight. It requires disciplined
decision-making under uncertainty.
Procedural legitimacy without judgment quality is incomplete legitimacy.
The point is not that every decision must produce a favorable outcome. A sound decision can produce a bad outcome under
uncertainty, and a weak decision can occasionally get lucky. The legitimacy question is whether the reasoning was appropriate to
the evidence available, whether confidence was calibrated, and whether the institution remained able to correct itself when reality
changed.

43. Capital Allocation Is a Judgment Function
Most investors are not operators inside the companies they fund. Their central function is allocation: deciding where capital should
go under uncertainty. A judgment metric for capital allocators should therefore evaluate the quality of the allocation decision itself,
not require investors to become hands-on builders.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE
If an institution's function is allocation, its legitimacy should be measured in part by the quality of its
allocation judgments.
Relevant questions include: What evidence existed at decision time? What risks were identified? What assumptions were made?

before the supposedly recognized capability became visible?

effects, macroeconomic conditions, and the advantages created by the allocation itself can all affect return.

46. Automation Can Increase the Value of Human Judgment
As automation absorbs routine monitoring, repetitive execution, and low-variance tasks, the work left to humans can become more

Automation can therefore make judgment more valuable at the same time institutions remain weak at

PROPOSED PRINCIPLE

remaining human decisions are disproportionately ambiguous, exceptional, and consequential.

those human judgments is assessed. Titles, tenure, credentials, and formal authority may remain relevant, but they are not
substitutes for observed judgment quality.
This reframes the human-in-the-loop question. The issue is not merely whether a human is present. It is whether the human role is

47. Capability Acceleration Raises the Governance Burden
Every increase in machine capability can create new relationships among permissions, dependencies, authorities, coupled
systems, downstream actors, and paths to consequence. The faster capability generalizes, the more important correction,
contestability, revocation, and contextual governance become.

control, correction, and revocation mechanisms.

state, not only point-in-time authorization.

48. Adaptation Lag
MIT's August 13, 2026 report describes generative AI as progressing across domains on a timescale too compressed for society to

49. Correctable Judgment
Good judgment under uncertainty is not the same as certainty. It is disciplined action that remains open to correction. An institution
can make a reasonable decision that later proves wrong. The legitimacy test is not whether error was impossible, but whether the
decision was evidence-aligned at the time and whether correction remained available after reality supplied new information.
Good judgment under uncertainty is disciplined action with preserved capacity for correction.
This principle creates a middle position between paralysis and overconfidence. Uncertainty cannot justify indefinite inaction, but
action under uncertainty should preserve observability, reversibility where feasible, escalation, and clear conditions for
re-evaluation.
The decision process therefore needs a correction path: what observation would trigger review, who has authority to revise the
decision, what evidence would count, and how prior assumptions are preserved so that learning is possible rather than rewritten
after the fact.
Correctability is a property of the decision architecture, not merely a personal virtue of the decision-maker.

50. Continuous Adequacy Review
A governance decision, assessment system, credentialing rule, operating policy, or institutional standard should not be treated as

Present reliance requires present adequacy. Historical validity should not silently become permanent

This is the institutional counterpart to the paper's historical-proof rule. It also prevents a common governance error: treating the

function embedded in the process.

55. Hidden Function Error
A process can perform several functions at once. If evaluation counts only the visible output, developmental, relational, governance,
succession, or judgment-forming functions can disappear from the accounting.
Visible activity
Possible hidden functions
Junior work
Skill formation, supervised judgment, succession, organizational memory
Manual review

Office hours / mentorship

Apprenticeship
Immediate contribution plus future capability and professional norms
Troubleshooting
Diagnosis skill, consequence exposure, system understanding
Peer collaboration
Communication, coordination, disagreement handling, shared context
PROPOSED TERM
Hidden Function Error: judging a process by one visible output while omitting other material functions that the process also
performs.
The error matters because optimization against the visible output can destroy the unmeasured functions. What appears to be
efficiency improvement can become capability erosion, governance weakening, or succession failure when the removed activity
was carrying more than one role.

56. Efficiency Destruction Test
Efficiency is not automatically beneficial if the removed activity is itself the mechanism producing the desired capability.
If the shortcut removes the mechanism that creates the capability, it is not merely a shortcut. It changes
what is being produced.
The Efficiency Destruction Test asks: What burden is being removed? What capability or relationship was formed through
that burden? Can the capability be formed another way? Does the new process preserve consequence exposure,
feedback, and practice?
This test applies beyond education to apprenticeships, debugging, inspection, troubleshooting, field experience, mentoring, junior
work, rehearsal, and other forms of learning-by-doing.
A process can be redesigned and still preserve function. The test does not require keeping historical forms for their own sake. It
requires accounting for the mechanism that produced the valued property before removing it.

PART XIX - CAPABILITY-PRESERVING AUTOMATION
57. Capability-Preserving Automation
MIT's 'augmentation not automation' principle argues for AI that helps people become more effective, tackle new tasks, and acquire
new expertise while preserving agency, judgment, integrity, and human connection. The relevant governance question is therefore
not simply whether a machine can perform the task.
The question is whether AI use expands or displaces the human capability the task was meant to
develop.
PROPOSED TERM
Capability-Preserving Automation: automation that reduces unnecessary burden while preserving or expanding the human
agency, judgment, learning, expertise, integrity, and connection relevant to the domain.
Automation should be evaluated not only by what labor it removes, but by what capability formation it also removes.
This creates a positive design target rather than a blanket anti-automation rule. A good automated system can increase access to
practice, reduce administrative friction, surface feedback faster, or let humans work on more consequential problems while still
maintaining meaningful exposure to reality and consequence.

58. Relational Function Loss
Study groups, mentorship, office hours, peer exchange, supervision, and apprenticeship may look like communication overhead
when measured only against immediate task completion. They can also be mechanisms of learning, trust, identity, judgment,
accountability, and community participation.
PROPOSED TERM
Relational Function Loss: the disappearance of developmental or governance value when automation removes interactions
whose contribution was not fully visible in the task-output metric.
When evaluating automation, measure what the process produces beyond the immediate output.
Relational function loss connects back to the paper's operator thesis. Hidden human contribution can be omitted from system
performance records; hidden human relationships can also be omitted from process redesign. In both cases, the metric records the
visible result while a causal contributor disappears.
This is not an argument that every meeting, mentorship structure, or human interaction must be preserved. It is an argument for
measuring the function before removing the form.

59. Context-Sensitive Governance
MIT argues that a uniform AI rule can be too permissive in one educational context and too restrictive in another. A first-year learner
developing foundational judgment stands in a different relationship to AI than an advanced researcher using AI to accelerate work in
a field already deeply understood.
PROPOSED PRINCIPLE
Governance should be calibrated to purpose, capability level, stakes, consequence, and context rather
than applied uniformly merely for administrative simplicity.
Context-sensitive governance does not mean every decision is ad hoc. Shared frameworks, disclosure expectations, accountability
standards, and common definitions can coexist with bounded local discretion.
The design problem is therefore layered: stable institutional principles at the top, context-sensitive implementation below, observed
execution at runtime, and reality-based review after outcomes occur.

60. Preserve Function, Not Necessarily Form
Institutional continuity does not always require preserving historical procedures. If a process, assessment, or rule no longer
performs its original function under changed conditions, preserving the form can undermine the function.
Preserve the function, not necessarily the form.
MIT makes this explicit by arguing that preserving what matters in education may require deliberate change. The same logic applies
to governance, credentialing, organizational design, and system evaluation.
61. Direct Observation and Dispute Clarification
Where an underlying property can be examined directly, direct observation should receive substantial evidentiary weight.
Governance disputes also become confused when different parties use the same language while evaluating different properties -
for example, safety as physical harm, privacy, misuse, speech, operational reliability, or institutional control.
Before resolving a governance dispute, identify the property each party is actually evaluating. Shared
vocabulary does not guarantee a shared question.

61.1. Interface-Function Separation
Digital systems make function-over-form especially visible. A software company may historically deliver
value through screens, menus, dashboards, or manual workflows. Agentic systems can shift interaction away
from those interfaces while increasing use of the underlying data, permissions, business logic, or service
capability. The disappearance of the interface therefore does not necessarily mean disappearance of the
function.
PROPOSED
TERM
Interface-Function Separation: the distinction between the visible form through which a
capability is accessed and the underlying function, service, authority, or value that the
form delivers.
• Interfaces are delivery mechanisms, not always the underlying product. A screen can disappear while the
service becomes more heavily used through another access layer.
• Institutional redesign should identify what must persist. Preserving a dashboard, form, classroom ritual, job
title, or bureaucratic step has little value if the underlying function has migrated elsewhere.
• Function migration changes measurement. Usage metrics tied to the old interface may decline while
underlying service demand grows, making interface-level metrics misleading.
• Governance must follow the function. Authority, auditability, provenance, accountability, and correction
cannot be allowed to disappear merely because execution moved behind an agent or abstraction layer.
When the interface stops being the product, identify the function the
institution was actually providing.
This extension connects software agents to the broader function-over-form principle without treating any single company
announcement as proof of a universal transition.

PART XXI - THE INTEGRATED EVALUATION ARCHITECTURE
62. From Proxy Discipline to Judgment Discipline
The integrated framework now has two layers. The first asks whether evidence is aligned to the property. The second asks whether
the institution making the decision maintains judgment worthy of continued reliance.
Step
Institutional evaluation requirement
1. Name the property
Capability, reliability, authority, safety, learning, allocation quality, or another defined property.
2. Name the state
What is true now, and what material change may have invalidated prior proof?
3. Select evidence
Use evidence capable of answering the defined question; separate screening from adjudication.
4. Preserve mechanism
Record operator rescue, productive friction, relational function, and resource effects that materially produce the outcome.
5. Make the decision
State the actual blocker, confidence, uncertainty, and what could change the decision.
6. Preserve correction
Define review triggers, revision authority, and the path for new evidence.
7. Revalidate
When capability, context, consequence, or production mechanism changes, re-establish measurement adequacy.
8. Learn from reality
Track outcomes and update the institution's judgment record.
The architecture is intentionally recursive. Evidence produces a decision; execution produces outcomes; outcomes produce new
evidence; new evidence can alter the institution's confidence, policy, measurement, or authority structure. Reality remains the final
correction surface.

63. The Legitimacy Loop
The paper began with a system receiving credit for an outcome actually preserved by an operator. The expanded framework shows
the same accounting problem at institutional scale.
System level: successful output can hide operator rescue. Credential level: familiar qualification can hide weak present
measurement. Capital level: successful return can hide how allocation changed the conditions of success. Governance level:
policy can hide lack of exercised control. Automation level: efficiency can hide lost capability formation. Institutional level:
authority can hide weak judgment.
The recurring failure is still proxy substitution: one observable thing is allowed to stand in for a different underlying property without
preserving the missing variables.
A legitimate institution therefore needs more than rules. It needs a judgment loop that remains connected to evidence, state,
consequence, correction, and present adequacy.
Authority grants the right to decide. Judgment determines whether the decision deserves continued
trust. Accountability determines whether bad judgment can be corrected.
The legitimacy loop does not eliminate disagreement. It makes disagreement inspectable by forcing the parties to identify the
property, evidence, uncertainty, reason, and correction rule actually at issue.

PART XXII - CONCLUSION
64. Conclusion: Reality First, Proxy Second
Modern institutions require shortcuts for evaluating complicated realities. They need credentials, metrics, benchmarks, intake
criteria, reputational signals, standards, references, governance policies, and investment heuristics. Those tools are not the enemy.
Confusing them with the realities they approximate is.
This paper began with a deceptively simple observation: sometimes a system appears to work because someone learned how to
compensate for the fact that it does not. Following that observation revealed a broader family of evaluation errors.
Sometimes a credential receives more weight than observed capability. Sometimes institutional familiarity receives more weight
than direct evidence. Sometimes a builder spends more resources making existing capability legible than developing the capability
itself. Sometimes capital is withheld pending proof that capital itself would help produce. Sometimes recognition accumulates
without allocation. Sometimes a policy exists without exercised control. Sometimes automation removes the mechanism that was
forming the very capability the institution later claims to value.
None of those conditions, by itself, proves bad faith. They reveal an evaluation problem.
The answer is not to eliminate institutional judgment. It is to make judgment more precise, observable, current, and correctable.
What is the question? What property is actually being evaluated? What evidence can establish it? What evidence cannot? What
condition could actually change the decision? Who or what actually produced the outcome? What changed since the prior proof?
What must remain true for the institution's judgment to stay worthy of reliance?
Reality first. Proxy second.
