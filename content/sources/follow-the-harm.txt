FOLLOW THE HARM
Proof, Power, and the Custody of Consequences in the Meta Youth-Safety
Settlement
A victim-centered examination of how human harm becomes evidence, evidence becomes public
authority, authority becomes bargaining leverage, and leverage becomes institutional value - while the
people at the beginning of that chain may remain unresolved at the end.
POSITION OF RECORD
This analysis does not align itself with Meta or with the state actors pursuing enforcement. Its primary
concern is the victims whose experiences, injuries, testimony, and evidence gave the underlying claims
their substance. Where the interests of Meta, the states, and the victims diverge, this paper evaluates
the outcome from the perspective of the victims first.

EXECUTIVE FINDING
CORE FINDING
The institutions obtained defined institutional outcomes. Many victims did not. That asymmetry is not
automatically unlawful and is not, by itself, proof of governance failure. The question is whether a legal
structure that intentionally separates public enforcement from individual representation contains
enough transparency, participation, remedy alignment, and accountability to keep the institutional
bargain connected to the people whose harm made the intervention necessary.
Meta agreed to a core multistate settlement with a maximum nominal value of approximately $16.68
billion, alongside related state settlements and reforms. The agreement creates a ten-year payment
structure, broad state releases, substantial youth-safety restrictions, and independent auditing. Meta
denies wrongdoing. [1][2]
At the same time, the settlement expressly preserves private individuals' claims. Thousands of personal-
injury matters remain unresolved, and Meta disclosed more than 200,000 mass-arbitration demands
relating to alleged social-media addiction and Instagram harms. [1][3]
The paper therefore does not ask the simplistic question, “Who won?” It asks a harder one: when alleged
human harm becomes evidence, authority, bargaining leverage, and money, who controls each transition,
what does each side gain or give up, and what mechanisms ensure that the final bargain remains
accountable to the people at the beginning of the chain?
1. WHY I LEANED IN
I saw Meta in the headlines over another scandal and did not think much of it at first. Big tech companies
are always getting dragged into something. Then I started seeing clips about the allegations, the
questioning, and what had allegedly been happening behind the scenes.
Then one number made me lean in: $1.4 trillion.
That was the possible exposure being discussed in the headlines, and I remember asking myself a simple
question: What did they do that the states believed could justify a number that large? I had never seen a
company discussed in connection with consumer-harm penalties on that scale.
The $1.4 trillion figure was not a forecast of what a jury would probably award. Meta characterized it as
the potential statutory exposure produced by four states' penalty theories. During trial, the states were
reported as seeking up to roughly $200 billion. Neither number was a final adjudicated damages award.
[4][5]
WHY THE $1.4T FIGURE MATTERS
Its significance is not that $1.4 trillion was likely to be collected. Its significance is that the states
advanced allegations and penalty theories at a scale capable of generating an extraordinary number.
That magnitude is a severity signal: it increases the public interest in understanding the underlying
conduct, the evidence, the decision-making, and why the public evidentiary process ended when it did.
I did not arrive at this question because of Meta. I arrived at Meta because the case made visible a pattern
I had watched for years: institutions can describe decisions as being made for the public while authority,

2. METHOD: FOLLOW THE CHAIN, THEN TRY TO DISPROVE THE CONCERN
The paper uses a custody-chain method. Each stage is treated as a distinct object with a distinct holder of
authority:
CUSTODY CHAIN
Human Harm -> Evidence -> Aggregated Evidence -> State Authority -> Legal Claim -> Litigation
Leverage -> Settlement Decision -> Financial Recovery -> Remedy Allocation -> Victim Benefit -> Reality
At each transition, the analysis asks:
 Who had custody of the information or value?
 Who had legal authority to make the next decision?
 Who received a direct benefit?
 Who surrendered rights, leverage, time, disclosure, or money?
 Who could object, enforce, revoke, or appeal?
 What was actually proven, and what remained alleged or unresolved?
 Did the outcome remain connected to the original human harm?
2.1 Falsifiability: the framework is not designed to condemn every public settlement
A predictable feature of parens patriae and consumer-protection law is that the state exercises public
authority without becoming each consumer's personal attorney. If this paper treated that separation alone
as proof of failure, the method would simply restate the law and reach the same conclusion in every case.
It therefore applies an adversarial test.
The governance concern weakens - and could be substantially falsified - where the record shows strong
mechanisms such as:
 formal victim consultation, objection, hearing, or participation rights appropriate to the legal vehicle;
 traceable direct restitution or a reasoned explanation for choosing population-level remediation
instead;
 transparent allocation rules and public accounting of where settlement money ultimately goes;
 a public explanation of why settlement served affected people better than continued adjudication;
 continued disclosure of material evidence where lawful and practicable;
 meaningful independent oversight and enforceable remedies;
 a demonstrated reduction in the underlying harm after implementation.
The issue, then, is not whether public and private claims are legally separate. The issue is whether that
lawful separation is well governed. A mechanism can operate exactly as designed and still be deficient in
participation, transparency, remedy alignment, or accountability.
Evidence labels used in this paper: ESTABLISHED means directly supported by a primary legal document,
statute, filing, or adjudication; SUPPORTED means supported by credible reporting or multiple sources;
OPEN means the public record does not presently establish the answer.

5. WHY $1.4 TRILLION MATTERED EVEN IF IT WAS ASPIRATIONAL
The $1.4 trillion figure appears unprecedented in modern U.S. consumer-protection enforcement against a
single company, although this paper deliberately avoids claiming a universal “largest ever” across every
civil filing in American history.

This comparison is not used to prove that Meta “really owed” $1.4 trillion. It does something different: it
establishes that the alleged conduct was being translated into an extraordinarily large public-enforcement
theory. When a legal theory reaches that scale, the underlying facts deserve deeper public examination,
not less.
SEVERITY SIGNAL, NOT VALUATION PROOF
The paper does not ask, “Why did the states settle for less than $1.4T?” as though $1.4T were a realistic
expected judgment. It asks: “What alleged conduct and scale of violation made a trillion-dollar theory
possible, and why did public adjudication stop before the evidentiary record was completed?”
6. THE REPRESENTATION GAP: LAWFUL BY DESIGN CAN STILL BE POORLY
GOVERNED
COPPA expressly authorizes a state attorney general, acting as parens patriae, to bring a civil action when
residents' interests are threatened or harmed and to seek injunctions, compliance, damages, restitution, or
other compensation on behalf of residents. [11]
The National Association of Attorneys General also explains that, although an attorney general may seek
consumer restitution, the attorney general does not represent and is not legal counsel for the individual
consumers. [12]
STRUCTURAL TENSION
The state can invoke injury to residents as a basis for enforcement authority without becoming those
residents' personal attorney or giving them ordinary client control over settlement decisions.
That separation is intentional legal design. The paper does not mistake it for an accidental defect. But
design status does not answer the governance question. Lawfulness tells us who may act; it does not
automatically prove that the resulting allocation of voice, money, evidence, and closure is well governed.

The Meta settlement makes the separation concrete. The consent judgment creates no private right of
action and gives non-parties no right to enforce the agreement. [1] At the same time, it expressly carves
out private individuals' claims for monetary or injunctive relief, meaning their separate cases continue.
[1]
No formal victim vote, opt-out, objection process, or approval mechanism comparable to a private class
settlement has been identified in the public settlement record. Whether particular attorneys general
privately consulted specific families during negotiations remains OPEN.
The strongest version of the critique is therefore not “parens patriae should not exist.” It is: when public
authority is justified through widespread citizen harm, what formal safeguards make the institutional
bargain accountable to the people whose harm created the case's human and evidentiary significance?

THE STRONGER QUESTION
Settlement may have been rational. Continued adjudication may also have been rational. The
governance issue is who had authority to choose between those paths, what evidence justified the
choice, and what procedural role - if any - the harmed population had in that exchange.
9. FOLLOW THE MONEY: REMEDIATION IS NOT THE SAME AS DIRECT
RESTITUTION
The core agreement requires the settling states to identify not less than 50% of covered payments as
compensatory restitution and remediation for federal reporting purposes, while also allowing certain
amounts for attorneys' fees, litigation costs, investigations, and other law-enforcement costs. [1]
That does not mean half of the settlement is automatically distributed to injured children or families.
“Remediation” can include population-level programs such as youth mental-health services, crisis
services, education, schools, digital wellness, and prevention programs. [1]
The state-specific custody rules differ materially. Examples from the agreement include Arizona
allocations described as remediation/restitution, California youth-remediation provisions alongside
specified governmental uses, Colorado authority for costs, attorneys' fees, restitution “if any,” enforcement
and public welfare, and Ohio allocations to its Consumer Protection Enforcement Fund. [1]

FOUR DIFFERENT OBJECTS
Direct victim restitution != population-level remediation != government enforcement spending !=
general governmental revenue. A public statement that collapses these categories into “money for
children” does not establish who actually receives the economic value.
This distinction is not unique to Meta. The Tobacco Master Settlement created enormous state recoveries
while leaving states substantial discretion over spending. [9] The recurring structure strengthens the
governance question rather than turning Meta into an isolated anomaly.
10. WHAT META BOUGHT - AND WHAT IT PAID
The settlement is an exchange, not merely a fine. Meta receives a defined ten-year payment framework,
broad releases of settling-state claims subject to carve-outs, termination of the active AG trial, reduced
catastrophic-verdict uncertainty, and a known compliance regime. [1][2] Meta shares rose after the
announcement, an external signal that investors valued the reduction in legal uncertainty. [2]
Meta also pays materially: billions in guaranteed and contingent obligations, product restrictions, age-
assurance requirements, usage limits, parental controls, independent auditing, compliance burdens, and
reputational cost. Private litigation continues. [1][2]
The relevant economic question is therefore not whether $16.68 billion is “a lot.” It is whether the present
value of Meta's payment and compliance burden is smaller or larger than the value of the legal,
evidentiary, reputational, and strategic uncertainty it removed. The public record does not permit a
complete calculation.
11. WHAT THE STATES BOUGHT - AND WHAT THEY SURRENDERED
The settling states receive guaranteed recovery without verdict or appellate risk, immediate nationwide
youth-safety restrictions, monitoring authority, institutional control over large pools of
remediation/enforcement funding, and a template capable of influencing other platforms. Those are
genuine public benefits.
In exchange, they surrendered the possibility of a larger judgment, additional public testimony and
evidence in the state trial, some future state claims covered by the release, and the opportunity for a full
merits adjudication in that proceeding.
THE HARD QUESTION
If the case was serious enough to support historically extraordinary liability theories and a settlement
the states themselves describe as historic, why was this the point where most states preferred
negotiated certainty to completed adjudication?
The public record supplies rational institutional answers: verdict risk, appeal risk, litigation cost,
guaranteed recovery, and immediate reforms. What it does not establish is whether that institutional
bargain was the best available outcome for victims. That proposition must be demonstrated, not assumed.

12. NEW MEXICO AND FLORIDA: COUNTERFACTUALS, NOT PROOF OF $1.4T
New Mexico is valuable because it provides an adjudicated comparator. In March 2026 a jury found 75,000
violations of New Mexico's Unfair Practices Act and imposed the statutory maximum $375 million civil
penalty. In August the court added $567 million and five years of court-supervised reforms, bringing total
financial exposure to $942 million. [6][7]
That result does not validate the $1.4 trillion theory. In fact, the difference between $942 million in one
state and a trillion-dollar theoretical exposure is a reason to keep the objects separate. New Mexico proves
something narrower and more useful: continuing through adjudication was a real path capable of
producing findings of liability, maximum statutory penalties, and structural relief.
Florida declined the national settlement and continued its litigation. That disagreement matters because it
shows negotiated closure was not the only position a state actor could reasonably take.
The comparators therefore serve two distinct functions: New Mexico shows what adjudication can
produce; Florida shows that institutional judgment about when to stop was contested. Neither establishes
that the other states would have recovered anything close to $1.4 trillion.
13. WHAT STOPPED BECOMING PUBLIC
Settlement ended an active mechanism through which internal evidence and sworn testimony were
becoming public. The consent judgment resolved the state action without final adjudication of the
underlying facts and law. [1]
The court had required each side to identify hundreds of priority exhibits, and senior leadership -
including Mark Zuckerberg - remained expected as witnesses. The complete final set of unreached exhibits
and the exact unreached examination cannot be reconstructed responsibly from the completed public
record.
The agreement creates meaningful independent auditing, including access to relevant data, systems,
internal documents, and communications for implementation review. But future compliance auditing asks
a different question from historical adjudication.
DIFFERENT PROOF OBJECTS
Trial: What happened? Who knew? What was unlawful? What consequence should follow?
Auditor: Is Meta implementing the negotiated restrictions now?
Both functions matter. They are not substitutes. Oversight can be strong while the historical record
remains incomplete.
The bargaining path is also deliberately opaque. The public can inspect the agreement, but not the
complete sequence of offers, counteroffers, internal valuations, or whether particular testimony or
exhibits changed the settlement value. That gap is stated as a gap, not filled with speculation.

15. PROOF OF WHAT?
The Meta case exposes a proof-claim contradiction. “Proof” changes meaning depending on the actor and
object.
Public claim What would actually prove it?
“Historic $18B settlement for children.” Guaranteed vs contingent value; exact recipient; permissible uses;
direct restitution vs remediation vs enforcement spending.
“The states represented harmed citizens.” Legal authority is established. Formal victim control, consultation,
consent, or objection rights are separate questions.
“Meta was held accountable.” Which claims were adjudicated? Which were settled without
admission? What restrictions are enforceable? What private cases
continue?
“Independent auditing ensures transparency.” Audit scope, access rights, public reporting, confidentiality rules, and
whether historical conduct is within scope.
“The victims won.” Direct compensation, safety improvement, procedural voice, time to
resolution, emotional burden, and whether harms are actually
reduced.
A statement can be rhetorically true and still be evidentially incomplete. The discipline is to identify the
proof object before accepting the proof claim.
16. THE AI BRIDGE: FROM META'S LATE GOVERNANCE TO PREVENTIVE
GOVERNANCE
The bridge to AI requires an argument, not an analogy. It proceeds in four steps.
Step 1 - Large systems optimize what they can measure.
Social platforms can optimize engagement, retention, recommendation performance, safety metrics, or
other measurable objectives. AI systems likewise optimize measurable proxies such as accuracy,
helpfulness, task completion, reward, latency, or user satisfaction.
Step 2 - Harm can accumulate outside the primary metric.
A system can improve on the metric it is designed to optimize while producing costs elsewhere: autonomy,
mental health, epistemic quality, discrimination, manipulation, privacy, or distributed economic risk. The

attached 2026 Accuracy Paradox paper independently makes this point for AI: factual accuracy alone can
miss epistemic unreliability, over-trust, sycophancy, manipulation, and social harms. [13]
Step 3 - If those harms are not measured early, humans become the detection mechanism.
In the Meta matter, alleged harms became legible through complaints, individual experiences,
investigations, discovery, litigation, expert evidence, and eventually government enforcement. That is late
detection. The system may have been operating and creating value long before the public accountability
mechanism became strong enough to react.
Step 4 - AI governance should move the detection point upstream.
The lesson is not “Meta proves AI is dangerous.” The lesson is that governance should test for divergence
between system benefit and human cost before accumulated injury becomes the evidence required to
force intervention.
PREVENTIVE GOVERNANCE PRINCIPLE
A trustworthy system should not require a population of victims before its harmful incentives become
visible. Governance should detect divergence between system benefit and human cost while
intervention is still preventive - not after harm has become sufficiently large to monetize through
litigation.
For AI systems, early danger zones include:
 performance metrics improving while human-welfare signals deteriorate;
 authority expanding faster than monitoring, revocation, or rollback capacity;
 small individual harms accumulating into population-level risk;
 outputs that are technically “not inaccurate” but manipulate, narrow, or over-persuade;
 economic benefit concentrating in the operator while risk is distributed to users;
 users lacking meaningful mechanisms to contest, stop, or obtain remedy from the system;
 claims of success being accepted without evidence that the claimed outcome actually occurred.
At the point where governance begins only after victims, lawsuits, regulators, and penalties appear,
institutions are no longer deciding how to prevent the harm. They are negotiating custody of the
consequences.

19. CONCLUSION: A SYSTEM CAN WORK AS DESIGNED AND STILL FAIL THE
PEOPLE IT CLAIMS TO PROTECT
This paper does not conclude that Meta secretly bribed officials or that the settling states acted unlawfully.
The public record does not establish those claims.
It also does not conclude that parens patriae authority is illegitimate merely because the state is not each
victim's personal lawyer. That separation is part of the legal design.
The harder finding is that legal design and good governance are not synonyms. A system can operate
exactly as intended while still leaving an accountability gap between the people harmed, the institution
exercising authority derived from that harm, and the corporation purchasing negotiated closure.
The state may rationally prefer guaranteed recovery and immediate reforms. Meta may rationally
purchase certainty. Future children may receive meaningful protection. Continued adjudication could
have imposed years of delay, cost, and appellate risk. All of those propositions can be true at the same
time.
And still: the people whose experiences gave the enforcement action its human significance may remain
outside the settlement table and inside continuing litigation.

FINAL TEST
Follow the harm. Follow the evidence. Follow the authority. Follow the money. Then ask whether they
ever return to the same person.
The $1.4 trillion figure was the reason to lean in, not because it proved a trillion dollars was recoverable,
but because a legal theory of that magnitude demanded a closer look at what had allegedly happened. The
investigation then exposed a broader problem: once human harm becomes institutional leverage, the
governance question is not only whether institutions had authority to act. It is whether the way they
exercised that authority remained accountable to the people whose harm made action necessary.
That is the test this paper applies to Meta. It is also the test AI governance should apply upstream - before
the next system produces enough victims for courts, regulators, and settlements to become the first
reliable feedback loop.

Source discipline
The consent judgment is the primary source for settlement terms. Reuters and other reporting are used for
trial timing, market reaction, and contemporaneous descriptions not contained in the agreement. New
Mexico DOJ sources establish the New Mexico adjudicated outcome. Federal statutes and NAAG materials
establish the public-enforcement framework. Historical comparisons use federal government sources
where available.
The attached 2026 Accuracy Paradox paper is used only for the AI-governance bridge: it argues that
factual accuracy alone is insufficient and that trustworthy governance must account for epistemic
reliability, manipulation, user autonomy, pluralism, uncertainty, and context. The Meta case analysis does
not rely on that paper to establish facts about Meta.
