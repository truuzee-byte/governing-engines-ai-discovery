Abstract
Human beings live inside two overlapping systems. The first is reality itself: matter, energy, biology,
space, time, causality, uncertainty, physical limitation, resource distribution, human capability, and
consequence. The second is the immense collection of abstractions humans have constructed to
coordinate life inside that reality: money, property, employment, corporations, governments, laws,
borders, credentials, markets, insurance, social status, schedules, metrics, and thousands of derivative
structures.
These abstractions are not imaginary in the sense of being inconsequential. They can determine where a
person may live, whether medical treatment is available, what resources a person may access, what
authority they possess, where they may travel, and what opportunities are available. Their
consequences are often extremely real. But real consequence does not establish reality-level necessity.
This paper develops a civilizational decomposition method for separating underlying reality-bound
responsibilities from the human mechanisms currently used to satisfy them. The method begins with an
ordinary day, strips away constructed layers, identifies the problems reality recreates anyway, maps
those problems to recurring civilizational responsibilities, and then audits major abstractions against
those responsibilities.
The paper does not begin from capitalism, socialism, markets, planning, centralized government,
decentralization, or abolition. Those are already bundled answers. The inquiry moves beneath them. Its
central question is: What does society claim must exist for civilization to function, and which of those
requirements are actually imposed by reality rather than inherited human architecture?
Executive Thesis
A structure can become indispensable to the functioning of an existing system
without being indispensable to the underlying reality that system is attempting to
govern.
Civilizations inherit mechanisms. Once those mechanisms become infrastructure, later generations
experience them less as design choices and more as environmental facts. A person born into a monetary
economy does not first encounter money as an optional coordination technology. They encounter it as a
condition of participation. A person born into nation-states does not first encounter jurisdiction as a
historical implementation. They encounter borders, citizenship, legal identity, and state authority as the
map of the world.
The analytical mistake occurs when dependence on an inherited mechanism is treated as proof that the
mechanism is fundamental. Human beings may need allocation; that does not establish every present
allocation mechanism. Humans may need productive activity; that does not establish wage employment
as the only viable coordination structure. Humans may need competence assurance; that does not
establish any particular credential. Humans may need conflict resolution; that does not establish every
feature of a contemporary legal system.
The purpose of Human Abstractions is not to destroy the map. It is to stop confusing the map with the
terrain.

1. The Inquiry
1.1 Two kinds of rules
Some rules belong to the world whether humans recognize them or not. Bodies require compatible
biological conditions. Physical work requires energy. Matter occupies space. Actions produce
consequences. Processes take time. Information can be incomplete. A bridge can fail even when every
person involved sincerely believes it is safe.
Other rules exist because humans created, recognized, recorded, enforced, or inherited them. A
mortgage has conditions. A corporation has governance. A university has admission requirements. A
country has citizenship rules. A market has accepted exchange protocols. A company has job titles. A
court has jurisdiction.
The second category is not unreal. Human enforcement can make a constructed rule materially
consequential. The distinction is therefore not real versus fake. It is reality-level constraint versus
human-created mechanism.
1.2 The governing question
If humans had never invented this abstraction, what problem would reality still
force us to solve?
If the problem disappears when the abstraction disappears, the problem may be substantially
abstraction-generated. If the problem remains but can be solved through multiple mechanisms, the
abstraction is an implementation. If the problem reappears because physical, biological, informational,
or causal conditions recreate it, the underlying responsibility is closer to the reality floor.
This removal test does not prove that an alternative will work at scale. It establishes the correct object of
investigation. Once the underlying responsibility is isolated, competing mechanisms can be evaluated
against the same responsibility rather than defended by tradition.
2. The Thursday Test: One Ordinary Day
6:00 AM - The Alarm
The alarm sounds because the person has somewhere to be. Human bodies require sleep. Travel takes
time. Events occur sequentially. Those conditions remain if clocks disappear. But 6:00 AM is a
standardized representation, Thursday is a calendar classification, and 'report to work at 8:00' is an
institutional requirement. Remove the clock and duration remains. Remove Thursday and astronomical
cycles continue. Remove the employment schedule and productive responsibilities remain, but reality
itself does not command a particular reporting time.
6:05 AM - The House
The person stands inside a structure that protects a human body from environmental conditions. Shelter
is reality-near. Yet access to the structure may be mediated by ownership, rent, mortgage, interest,
insurance, zoning, property tax, title, lease, utility accounts, and legal occupancy. If the person's bank
balance falls to zero, the roof does not lose its physical ability to stop rain. What changes is institutional
permission to remain beneath it.

6:20 AM - Water and Electricity
Water must physically exist, be safe enough for use, and reach the person. Electricity requires
generation, transmission, infrastructure, maintenance, and energy. Those responsibilities remain if
billing disappears. Customer accounts, rates, meters, late fees, and disconnection rules are mechanisms
governing the delivery architecture. The audit asks which parts are required to deliver the resource and
which are required to operate the current institutional model of delivery.
6:45 AM - Breakfast
Biology requires nutrients and energy. Modern access may pass through agriculture, land ownership,
financing, labor, distribution, retail, price, banking, and purchasing power. The biological endpoint is
nutrition. The institutional endpoint may be ability to pay. A resource can physically exist while a
person remains institutionally unable to access it.
7:15 AM - Clothing
Clothing can protect the body from environmental conditions while simultaneously carrying dress
codes, professional norms, status signals, fashion, gender conventions, branding, and organizational
requirements. One object can therefore satisfy both reality-bound and abstract responsibilities. Human-
created does not mean meaningless.
7:30 AM - The Vehicle
The vehicle is governed by physics and institutions at once. Momentum, traction, mass, reaction time,
and collision severity are physical relationships. Title, registration, licensing, insurance, financing, traffic
law, and jurisdiction are constructed mechanisms. A speed limit is not a law of physics, but it may
govern a genuine relationship between speed and harm. The mechanism is constructed; the condition it
attempts to govern may not be.
7:45 AM - Invisible Lines
The person crosses a municipal or county boundary. Nothing visible changes in the soil or atmosphere,
yet different authorities, taxes, procedures, and regulations may apply. An abstraction does not require
physical embodiment to exercise causal power through humans who recognize and enforce it.
8:00 AM - Work
Civilization requires productive activity: food, maintenance, care, energy, construction, knowledge,
repair. Employment surrounds productive activity with employer, employee, wage, schedule,
management, benefits, payroll, tax withholding, performance rules, and organizational authority. Work
survives conceptual removal of employment. Employment does not automatically survive conceptual
removal of work.
10:30 AM - The Manager
A manager's authority is not a biological property. It is delegated through an organizational chain. Yet
refusal can affect employment, employment can affect income, and income can affect food, shelter,
healthcare, and mobility. Authority in one abstraction can propagate through dependencies into
unrelated domains.

12:00 PM - Lunch
Biology returns. The body consumes energy and requires more. Human abstractions repeatedly mediate
access to the satisfaction of recurring biological conditions. The fact that a requirement returns
physically does not establish that the same access mechanism must mediate it each time.
1:30 PM - Productivity
The employer measures performance. A condition becomes a measurement, the measurement becomes
a metric, the metric becomes a target, and the target becomes attached to reward or punishment. At that
point the representation begins reshaping the reality it was created to describe.
5:00 PM - Payday
Numbers change in a database. The person's body may be unchanged, but access to food, shelter,
transportation, services, debt reduction, and discretionary goods changes. A socially recognized
representation has altered material possibility.
5:30 PM - Grocery Store
The store contains physical objects capable of satisfying biological needs. The checkout system generally
asks whether the recognized monetary claim attached to the buyer can satisfy the recognized monetary
claim attached to the goods. Biological eligibility and institutional eligibility are different conditions.
6:30 PM - Healthcare
A biological condition can be surrounded by provider classification, scheduling, licensing, insurance,
coverage, network status, deductible, procedure code, billing, and payment. Some intermediary
responsibilities may be essential. Others may be products of surrounding abstractions. The point is to
separate the body-needs-treatment chain from the institution-allows-treatment chain.
8:00 PM - Success
Social comparison introduces abstractions that may no longer require external enforcement. Wealth,
career, followers, titles, degrees, marriage, homeownership, and prestige can become internal measures
of human worth. External structure becomes social expectation; social expectation can become self-
governance.
10:30 PM - Strip the Day Down
Remove dollars, rent, mortgages, corporations, job titles, credit scores, jurisdictions, insurance policies,
salaries, prices, degrees, KPIs, follower counts, and the word Thursday. A living organism remains. It still
requires water, nutrition, sleep, environmental protection, care, information, relationships, physical
resources, coordination, and mechanisms for handling conflict and uncertainty. Consequences remain.

5. The Abstraction Audit Method
A. Name the abstraction
Define the thing being examined without smuggling in a judgment.
B. Identify its claimed function
What problem does society say this abstraction solves?
C. Identify the underlying responsibility
What must still be accomplished if the abstraction disappears?
D. Locate the reality constraint
Which physical, biological, informational, causal, or human limitation produces the responsibility?
E. Map dependencies in
What other abstractions must exist for this abstraction to operate?
F. Map dependencies out
What later structures become necessary because this abstraction exists?
G. Record consequences
What does the mechanism actually cause, including effects outside its own accounting boundary?
H. Examine historical variants
Has the responsibility been handled differently under other conditions?

I. Generate alternative mechanisms
Could the same responsibility be satisfied through another architecture?
J. Perform the removal test
What breaks when the abstraction is removed? Which breakages are fundamental and which are local
to the surrounding system?
K. Identify the irreducible remainder
What problem survives every removal attempted so far?
L. Assign Reality Status
Reality-Bound, Implementation, Derived Dependency, Representation, Hybrid, or Unresolved.
6. Reality Status Taxonomy
REALITY-BOUND
The underlying requirement survives abstraction removal because reality recreates the condition.
IMPLEMENTATION
A real responsibility exists, but the abstraction is one human mechanism for satisfying it.
DERIVED DEPENDENCY
Its necessity arises substantially from other human abstractions.
REPRESENTATION
It describes, measures, symbolizes, or standardizes a condition without being the underlying
phenomenon.
HYBRID
The abstraction contains multiple layers or performs multiple classes of function.
UNRESOLVED
The decomposition is not sufficient to justify a stronger classification.
7. Local Necessity Versus Fundamental Necessity
Nearly every mature institution can demonstrate that removing it abruptly would create disruption.
That is not surprising. Systems are built around their existing components.
Local necessity asks whether a component is required if the surrounding architecture remains
unchanged. Fundamental necessity asks whether the underlying problem reappears even after
surrounding human abstractions are removed.
Payroll can be locally necessary to a wage-employment system. Claims processing can be locally
necessary to an insurance system. A property registry can be locally necessary to a title-based property

8. Abstraction Chains, Loops, and Dependency
Employment loop
Necessities -> Money -> Income -> Employment -> Continued access to necessities.
Housing loop
Property -> Housing price -> Mortgage or rent -> Income -> Credit/eligibility -> Housing access.
Credential loop
Credential -> Employment eligibility -> Income -> Education affordability -> Credential access.
Capital loop
Capital -> Productive capacity -> Profit -> More capital -> Greater productive capacity.
Authority loop
Political authority -> Law -> Enforcement -> Recognition of political authority.
A self-reinforcing loop can create evidence of its own necessity. If survival access is attached to job-
derived income, people will correctly report that they need jobs. That establishes a dependency in the
present architecture. It does not establish employment as a law of nature.
9. Abstraction Debt
Abstraction Debt is the additional coordination, administration, enforcement, corrective, and
maintenance machinery required because prior human abstractions introduced dependencies that must
themselves be managed.
Money enables generalized exchange and then requires mechanisms for fraud, accounting, banking
controls, financial regulation, identity verification, debt collection, bankruptcy, tax accounting, and
compliance. Ownership enables durable custody and exclusion and then requires title systems,
registries, liens, foreclosure processes, property litigation, and enforcement. Employment coordinates
productive labor and then generates payroll, benefits administration, labor regulation, unemployment
systems, workplace classification, and human-resources machinery.
These descendants may perform legitimate responsibilities. The analytical requirement is simply to
charge their existence to the architecture that generated them when comparing mechanisms. An
abstraction should not receive credit only for the problem it solves while the systems required to
maintain its consequences are treated as unrelated.

10. Abstraction Capture
Abstraction Capture occurs when a representation or mechanism created to serve an underlying
condition becomes the dominant optimization target and the underlying reality becomes subordinate to
it.
The recurring structure is: Reality -> Representation -> Target -> Reward or Authority -> Behavior ->
Changed Reality.
Learning can become a test score, after which teaching optimizes the score. Human attention can
become engagement, after which products optimize engagement. Organizational health can become a
KPI, after which behavior optimizes the KPI. Economic activity can become a financial metric, after
which policy and capital allocation optimize the metric.
The representation may remain useful. The failure occurs when improvement in the representation is
allowed to certify improvement in the underlying condition without checking the underlying condition.

12. Full Decomposition I - Money
12.1 What money is
Money is a socially recognized representation of claims. A dollar is not food, shelter, electricity, labor, or
a machine. It is a generalized claim that participating institutions and people agree to recognize under
specified conditions.
12.2 Representation
Money allows heterogeneous goods, services, time, and obligations to be expressed through a common
representation. The underlying responsibility is not 'have dollars'; it is scalable representation of
transferable claims.
12.3 Accounting
Civilizations need to track resource flows, obligations, production, consumption, and tradeoffs.
Monetary accounting compresses many dimensions into a common unit. Accounting is a responsibility;
monetary accounting is an implementation.
12.4 Exchange coordination
Money reduces the need for direct coincidence of wants. A person can contribute to one actor and later
redeem the recognized claim with another. The underlying responsibility is scalable exchange among
actors who may not know one another.
12.5 Scarcity signaling
Prices can communicate information about demand, availability, production difficulty, and competing
uses. But price is not physical scarcity. Monopoly, policy, speculation, information asymmetry, and
market power can alter price. The signal is generated by a mechanism.
12.6 Allocation
Markets often resolve competing claims through ability to pay. That is a real allocation mechanism, but
monetary capacity is not identical to biological need, social consequence, or moral priority.
12.7 The ability-to-pay gate
A resource can physically exist and remain institutionally inaccessible because the claimant lacks the
accepted monetary representation. Physical availability and institutional eligibility must therefore be
tracked separately.
12.8 Work and income
Reality may require productive contribution. The structure sell labor -> receive money -> purchase
access to necessities is one implementation of that requirement, not the requirement itself.
12.9 Incentives
Money is a generalized incentive token because it can be converted into many desired outcomes. The
underlying responsibility is to ensure necessary work is performed reliably, including work that is
unpleasant, dangerous, repetitive, or otherwise unattractive.

12.10 Stored claims
Savings preserve claims across time. The underlying responsibility is continuity between present
contribution and future access. That does not independently establish unlimited accumulation or every
inheritance and compounding rule.
12.11 Capital
Large monetary claims can purchase productive capacity: land, machines, computation, labor,
distribution, research, and companies. Money can therefore become a claim over future possibility, not
merely current consumption.
12.12 Debt
Debt permits present access in exchange for future obligation. It makes future production partially
claimable by past agreements. The responsibility may be legitimate; the architecture remains human-
created.
12.13 Creditworthiness
Credit scores arise because lending architectures need scalable estimates of repayment risk. They can be
locally useful while remaining derived dependencies of a particular financial mechanism.
12.14 Interest
Interest compensates for combinations of time, risk, opportunity cost, inflation, default possibility, and
profit. The deeper question is how benefits from previously accumulated resources that enable new
production should be allocated.
12.15 Wealth
Real productive wealth can include food capacity, housing, energy, healthcare, infrastructure,
knowledge, machines, healthy ecosystems, and free human time. Monetary wealth represents claims
against parts of that world. The representation can rise while some underlying human conditions
deteriorate.
12.16 Profit
Profit compares monetarily recognized revenue with monetarily recognized cost. Real consequences can
fall outside the accounting boundary. Profitable therefore means the accepted monetary accounting
shows a surplus; it does not independently establish net human or ecological benefit.
12.17 Greed and unbounded representation
Biological consumption has practical limits. A generalized numerical claim can grow without an
intrinsic biological stopping point. Money can therefore provide an unusually scalable vehicle for
accumulation, although money and greed are not identical.
12.18 Human worth
Monetary holdings cannot directly establish wisdom, kindness, creativity, moral character, parental
value, dignity, or competence outside the domain actually measured. Converting economic
representation into generalized human ranking is a category error.

12.19 Necessities
Using the same allocation mechanism for discretionary consumption and biological necessities creates a
condition in which a person can be monetarily ineligible for something they remain biologically eligible
for.
12.20 Four kinds of scarcity
Physical Scarcity
The resource itself is insufficient, inaccessible, unusable, or physically unavailable.
Allocative Scarcity
Available quantity cannot satisfy all simultaneous claims.
Access Scarcity
The resource exists, but an actor lacks the institutionally accepted claim required for access.
Artificial Scarcity
Supply or access is deliberately restricted by human mechanism despite greater physical availability
being possible.
12.21 Money Reality Separation Record
Primary classification: IMPLEMENTATION / BUNDLED MECHANISM / DEPENDENCY ROOT.
Underlying responsibilities: exchange, accounting, allocation, scarcity signaling, transferable claims,
deferred claims, incentives, stranger coordination, capital aggregation.
Reality-bound conditions: heterogeneous needs and capabilities, uneven resource distribution, rivalrous
resources, finite productive capacity, uncertainty, coordination requirements, and projects requiring
pooled resources.
Fundamental necessity of the current implementation: NOT ESTABLISHED.
Necessity of several underlying responsibilities: STRONGLY SUPPORTED.
Money's usefulness does not establish the necessity of every dependency
civilization has attached to money.
13. Full Decomposition II - Ownership and Property
13.1 The bundle
Ownership commonly bundles use, exclusion, decision authority, transfer, income rights, stewardship,
liability, inheritance, and sometimes destruction rights. Those functions need not be treated as one
indivisible thing.
13.2 Reality underneath property
Physical resources occupy space, can be damaged, require maintenance, and can face incompatible
claims. Those conditions create real responsibilities around custody, stewardship, allocation, and
conflict.

Governing Engines | Human Abstractions v1.0 | Public Paper
13.3 Use is not title
A human can physically use a resource without holding a recognized title. Title is a representation of an
institutional claim about authority over use and transfer.
13.4 Stewardship
Someone or some system must often maintain resources. This is one of the strongest reality-bound
responsibilities beneath property. The question is whether stewardship requires the entire
contemporary ownership bundle.
13.5 Exclusion
Some uses are incompatible. Exclusion can therefore be a legitimate coordination mechanism. But the
need to resolve incompatible uses does not establish unlimited exclusion across all resource classes.
13.6 Land
Land is physical; parcel boundaries, title, zoning classifications, and market valuation are human
overlays. Geography survives. The jurisdictional and ownership map does not automatically.
13.7 Inheritance
Generational continuity is real. The transfer of knowledge, care, responsibility, and infrastructure must
occur. The automatic transfer of every property claim under a particular inheritance regime is a
separate design choice.
13.8 Removal test
Remove ownership conceptually. Questions remain: Who may use a resource? Who maintains it? Who
resolves incompatible claims? Who bears consequences? The responsibilities survive. The current
bundle is therefore best treated as an implementation until stronger necessity is demonstrated.
Ownership may implement genuine responsibilities without every present
ownership rule becoming reality-bound.
14. Full Decomposition III - Work and Employment
14.1 Productive activity
Civilization requires food production, care, maintenance, construction, logistics, research, energy, and
other work. Productive activity survives institutional removal.
14.2 Employment as coordination
Employment packages role assignment, authority, scheduling, compensation, accountability, and
organizational membership.
14.3 Native versus attached functions
Native functions include coordinating labor, assigning responsibility, and compensating contribution.
Attached functions can include healthcare access, retirement access, housing eligibility,
creditworthiness, and social status.

14.4 Dependency amplification
When unrelated necessities are attached to employment-derived income, job loss propagates beyond the
loss of one productive role.
14.5 Incentive problem
Necessary work still has to occur under any alternative. A credible redesign must answer who performs
undesirable work, how competence is established, how contribution is coordinated, and how free-riding
or chronic nonparticipation is handled.
14.6 Automation
Automation can reduce the amount of human labor required for a responsibility, but it does not
eliminate the responsibility itself. Machines require design, energy, maintenance, monitoring, and
correction.
14.7 Removal test
Remove employment. Production, specialization, coordination, competence, responsibility, and
incentives remain. Employment is therefore an implementation bundle rather than the underlying
requirement.
Reality may require productive activity without requiring the institution of
employment.
15. Full Decomposition IV - Government, Authority, and Law
15.1 Government as a bundle
Government commonly carries coordination, infrastructure, collective decision-making, authority,
conflict resolution, security, shared-resource governance, financing, and consequence assignment.
15.2 Governing responsibility versus government form
Large groups need ways to make collective decisions, assign authority, preserve continuity, and resolve
conflicts. Those responsibilities can survive removal of a particular state architecture.
15.3 Authority requires scope
An authority that applies to everything without boundary is structurally different from bounded
authority. Scope may be territorial, functional, contractual, resource-based, or otherwise defined.
Authority scope appears important; nation-state borders are one implementation.
15.4 Law
Remove statute books and humans can still harm one another, make agreements, dispute claims, and
require adjudication. Conflict resolution, behavioral predictability, and consequence remain. Specific
legal classifications and procedures are implementations.

15.5 Enforcement
Rules without any mechanism of consequence may fail to govern actors who reject them. Enforcement is
therefore a genuine design problem. Police, courts, sanctions, restorative systems, technical constraints,
and community mechanisms are different implementations.
15.6 Legitimacy
Authority can be obeyed because of force, consent, tradition, perceived competence, procedure,
dependency, or social recognition. These are different sources of practical legitimacy and should not be
collapsed.
15.7 Removal test
Remove a particular government. Coordination, shared infrastructure, conflict, harm, collective
decisions, external threats, and authority questions remain. Government form is implementation;
governing responsibilities survive.
16. Full Decomposition V - Territory, Borders, and Membership
16.1 Geography versus jurisdiction
Mountains, rivers, distance, climate, and resource distribution are physical. Jurisdictional boundaries
assign human meaning and authority to portions of geography.
16.2 Membership
Groups need ways to distinguish participants, responsibilities, rights, and obligations. Citizenship is one
membership architecture.
16.3 Movement
Physical movement is constrained by distance, terrain, energy, and safety. Immigration law adds
institutional permission to movement across jurisdictional boundaries.
16.4 Security
Communities may face genuine external threats. That does not by itself establish any specific border
regime.
16.5 Removal test
Remove national borders conceptually. Geography, logistics, group identity, coordination, security, and
authority scope remain. The current territorial implementation is therefore hybrid rather than identical
to geography itself.
17. Full Decomposition VI - Education, Knowledge, and Credentials
17.1 Learning survives school
Humans require knowledge transmission and competence development. A school is an institutional
implementation.

[Source PDF page 19]
Governing Engines | Human Abstractions v1.0 | Public Paper
17.2 Knowledge custody
Civilizations must preserve discoveries across individual lifespans. Libraries, archives, universities,
journals, oral traditions, databases, and digital systems are mechanisms.
17.3 Competence versus credential
Competence is the ability to perform within a relevant domain. A credential is a representation of an
institutional judgment about competence.
17.4 Trust at scale
Credentials reduce the cost of individually re-testing every person. This is a real coordination advantage.
17.5 Representation failure
A credentialed person can be incompetent and an uncredentialed person can be capable. When the
representation conflicts with demonstrated reality, the representation should not automatically win.
17.6 Social sorting
Education systems can perform learning and social sorting simultaneously. Admission, grades, prestige,
and degree hierarchy can affect access far beyond knowledge itself.
17.7 Removal test
Remove schools and degrees. Learning, knowledge preservation, child development, competence
formation, and competence verification remain. The institutional forms are implementations.
Knowledge requirement is not credential requirement.
18. Full Decomposition VII - Healthcare and Insurance
18.1 Biological care
Bodies become injured, infected, diseased, disabled, and old. Care is reality-bound.
18.2 Expertise
Some interventions require specialized knowledge, equipment, sterile environments, and coordinated
teams. Those requirements remain regardless of payment mechanism.
18.3 Insurance
Uncertain losses can exceed individual capacity. Risk pooling is a legitimate responsibility. Commercial
insurance is one implementation.
18.4 Institutional chain
Biological condition -> provider -> scheduling -> licensing -> coverage -> network -> authorization ->
intervention -> coding -> billing -> payment. The audit asks which links protect care quality and resource
coordination and which primarily maintain surrounding financial architecture.

[Source PDF page 20]
Governing Engines | Human Abstractions v1.0 | Public Paper
18.5 Eligibility
A biological condition can exist independently of insurance status. Coverage therefore governs
institutional access, not the existence of the medical need.
18.6 Removal test
Remove insurance. Illness, uncertain cost, expensive interventions, capacity constraints, triage, and risk
distribution remain. Insurance is an implementation of those responsibilities, not the biological
requirement itself.
19. Full Decomposition VIII - Corporations and Organization
19.1 Persistent productive entities
Complex projects can outlive individual participants. Civilization needs ways to preserve objectives,
assets, responsibilities, and knowledge through human turnover.
19.2 Coordination at scale
Organizations align specialized people, tools, information, authority, and dependencies.
19.3 Corporation as legal bundle
A corporation combines persistent identity, ownership claims, liability rules, governance, capital
aggregation, contracts, and succession.
19.4 Personhood and representation
Legal personhood is a coordination abstraction. It allows an entity to own, contract, sue, be sued, and
persist. It should not be confused with biological personhood.
19.5 Removal test
Remove the corporation. Large-scale production, persistent coordination, capital aggregation,
responsibility custody, and succession remain. Corporate form is one architecture for those
responsibilities.
20. Full Decomposition IX - Markets
20.1 What markets do
Markets coordinate exchange, reveal some preference information, generate prices, allocate through
bids and purchasing power, and create incentives for production.
20.2 Information value
Distributed price signals can carry useful information without any central actor possessing complete
knowledge.

[Source PDF page 21]
Governing Engines | Human Abstractions v1.0 | Public Paper
20.3 Market outcome is not human optimum
A market can clear while biological need remains unmet. It can allocate to purchasing power rather
than urgency, long-term consequence, or public value.
20.4 Externalized consequence
When consequences fall outside the transaction, the price may not represent the full real cost.
20.5 Removal test
Remove markets. Exchange, preference discovery, scarcity information, production incentives, and
allocation remain. Markets are a powerful mechanism bundle, not the underlying responsibilities
themselves.
21. Full Decomposition X - Measurement, Metrics, and Status
21.1 Measurement
Humans need ways to compare conditions, detect change, and coordinate standards. Measurement is
deeply useful.
21.2 Representation
A kilometer is human-defined; distance is not. A second is standardized; duration is not. Representation
can be constructed while accurately tracking a real phenomenon.
21.3 Metric governance
When rewards and authority depend on a metric, the metric becomes causally active.
21.4 Status
Functional differences in competence or responsibility can become generalized social ranking. Role-
specific authority should not be confused with generalized human worth.
21.5 Removal test
Remove rankings and prestige systems. Competence differences, coordination, trust, and role
assignment remain. Generalized status hierarchy does not automatically.
22. Technology and AI as Human Abstractions
Technology often feels closer to reality because it is built from physical machinery and mathematics. Yet
technological architectures are still human constructions inside reality. A transformer architecture,
chatbot interface, software platform, account model, agent framework, data pipeline, or training regime
is not a law of nature merely because it currently dominates an industry.
The same analytical rule applies: identify the responsibility first. What does the technology need to
accomplish? Which constraints are imposed by computation, energy, information, latency, uncertainty,
or hardware? Which constraints arise because the current architecture was designed a particular way?

[Source PDF page 22]
Governing Engines | Human Abstractions v1.0 | Public Paper
This distinction matters because technological history can repeat the same civilizational mistake: a
successful implementation becomes infrastructure, infrastructure becomes dependency, and
dependency becomes the boundary of imagination.
Human invention should remain subordinate to the reality it is intended to navigate. A system can claim
completion; reality can show failure. A model can produce a confident answer; evidence can contradict
it. A platform can optimize engagement; human well-being can move in the opposite direction.
23. The Civilizational Stack
Layer 0 - Reality
Matter, energy, biology, space, time/sequence, causality, uncertainty, resource distribution, human
capacities, and consequence.
Layer 1 - Reality-Generated Responsibilities
Survival, production, allocation, coordination, learning, protection, conflict resolution, maintenance,
resilience, continuity.
Layer 2 - Human Mechanisms
Exchange, authority, allocation, trust, identity, knowledge, incentive, and continuity mechanisms.
Layer 3 - Institutionalized Abstractions
Money, government, property, markets, employment, corporations, schools, banks, law, insurance.
Layer 4 - Derived Abstractions
Credit scores, mortgages, paychecks, political parties, grades, job titles, insurance networks, property
taxes, retirement accounts.
Layer 5 - Human Dependency
Food, housing, healthcare, mobility, opportunity, security, and participation become conditioned by the
abstraction stack.
Layer 6 - Internalization
The mechanism is experienced as reality: 'You need a job.' 'You need good credit.' 'That is just how the
economy works.'
24. Human Worth and the Migration of Abstraction
One of the most consequential abstraction failures occurs when a representation created for one domain
migrates into judgments about the whole human being.
Income can become a proxy for success. Credentials can become a proxy for intelligence. Job title can
become a proxy for importance. Nationality can become a proxy for belonging. Wealth can become a
proxy for competence. Popularity can become a proxy for value.

[Source PDF page 23]
Governing Engines | Human Abstractions v1.0 | Public Paper
The representation may contain information relevant to its original domain. The error is allowing it to
claim authority outside that domain without evidence.
A society that forgets this distinction can begin organizing human dignity around representations that
were never designed to measure it.
25. Scarcity, Abundance, and Access
This framework does not assume abundance. Some resources are genuinely finite, geographically
concentrated, time-sensitive, capacity-limited, or rivalrous. A credible civilization must govern scarcity
rather than wish it away.
But scarcity should be decomposed. A shortage produced by physical absence is different from a
shortage produced by production limits, distribution failure, access rules, strategic withholding, or
inability to satisfy a monetary claim.
The destination called abundance does not logically require preserving every historical route used to
allocate scarce goods. If technology changes production capacity, information availability, coordination
cost, or automation, mechanisms built under earlier constraints should be re-evaluated against the new
reality.
26. The Ordinary Claim 'Society Needs X'
When someone says society needs money, jobs, government, property, markets, degrees, insurance,
corporations, or borders, the statement usually compresses several different claims.
It may mean the present system would fail if X disappeared tomorrow. That is a local-necessity claim.
It may mean X currently performs a responsibility that truly must be performed. That is a function
claim.
It may mean no alternative mechanism could perform that responsibility at acceptable cost and scale.
That is a much stronger comparative claim.
It may mean the underlying problem itself cannot be removed because reality recreates it. That is a
fundamental-necessity claim.
These claims require different evidence. Treating them as interchangeable is one reason debates about
institutions become circular.
27. Design Principles for Rebuilding From the Floor
Responsibility before mechanism
Identify what must be accomplished before selecting the institution that will accomplish it.
Reality before ideology
A preferred theory does not override physical, biological, informational, or causal constraints.
Separate access from availability
Track whether a resource is absent or merely institutionally inaccessible.

[Source PDF page 24]
Governing Engines | Human Abstractions v1.0 | Public Paper
Separate representation from phenomenon
A score, price, credential, title, or metric cannot certify the underlying condition by itself.
Separate work from employment
Do not treat productive responsibility and one labor-coordination mechanism as identical.
Separate authority from structure
Authority may be necessary in some contexts without validating every inherited authority architecture.
Account for abstraction debt
Charge downstream maintenance and correction machinery to the abstractions that generate it.
Preserve uncertainty
If decomposition is incomplete, classify the question as unresolved rather than forcing a conclusion.
Require removal tests
Ask what actually breaks when a mechanism is removed and why.
Revalidate under changed capability
A mechanism justified under old technological or productive constraints may not remain optimal when
those constraints change.
Do not confuse disruption with impossibility
Transition cost is real but different from fundamental impossibility.
Reality retains veto
Any replacement must survive consequences, not merely argument.
28. What This Paper Does Not Claim
It does not claim that every abstraction is harmful.
It does not claim that constructed institutions are unreal or inconsequential.
It does not claim that removing money, government, property, markets, employment, or credentials
tomorrow would produce a better society.
It does not claim that small historical communities prove that the same mechanism will scale to billions
of people.
It does not claim that technology eliminates scarcity, conflict, human limitation, or uncertainty.
It does not claim that all social classifications are fabricated in the same way; some abstractions may
combine observed physical or historical phenomena with constructed boundaries and assigned
meanings.
It does not prescribe a final replacement civilization in this first paper.

[Source PDF page 25]
Governing Engines | Human Abstractions v1.0 | Public Paper
Its narrower claim is that inherited implementation should not be granted reality-level necessity before
decomposition.
29. The Civilizational Audit Program
Human Abstractions is best understood as the beginning of an audit rather than a completed political
theory.
The program is to take the structures humanity commonly says must exist for civilization to function;
identify the responsibilities each structure performs; locate the reality conditions underneath those
responsibilities; map dependencies created by the structure; examine alternatives and historical
variants; perform removal tests; preserve unresolved questions; and compare mechanisms against the
reality they are intended to serve.
The method is deliberately capable of returning 'keep it.' An inherited mechanism may remain the
strongest known solution after decomposition. The point is not to force novelty. The point is to require
justification at the correct layer.

Governing Engines | Human Abstractions v1.0 | Public Paper
30. Conclusion - Remember Which Rules Are Ours
Human civilization did not arrive fully formed. Its institutions were constructed across generations by
people responding to the knowledge, technology, conflicts, fears, opportunities, and constraints available
to them.
Those structures deserve neither automatic rejection nor automatic permanence. They deserve
examination.
A society can become so dependent on its own machinery that it forgets the machinery was built. Once
that happens, redesign begins to sound impossible not because reality forbids the alternative, but
because the inherited mechanism defines the boundaries of imagination.
The corrective is not fantasy. It is stricter allegiance to reality.
Reality determines that humans require food. Humans determine how access is organized. Reality
determines that conflicting claims occur. Humans determine how they are adjudicated. Reality
determines that productive activity is necessary. Humans determine how production is coordinated and
rewarded. Reality determines that knowledge is incomplete. Humans determine how expertise is
recognized and corrected. Reality determines that actions have consequences. Humans determine which
institutions attempt to govern them.
The world establishes a possibility space. Humans construct institutions inside it. Those institutions can
govern human behavior through agreement, authority, dependency, or force. They do not thereby
become superior to the reality from which their responsibilities originated.
The first task is not to design the perfect replacement civilization. The first task is to see the current one
clearly.
Take an ordinary Thursday. Peel away every rule humanity added. Find what
remains. Then, for every structure we put back, ask why.
Inheritance is not proof of necessity.
Human beings may invent freely. Reality retains veto authority.

Appendix C - Public Reading Guide
This paper should be read as an analytical framework rather than a completed blueprint for a
replacement society.
When the paper classifies an abstraction as an implementation, it means the underlying responsibility
appears separable from the present mechanism. It does not mean the mechanism has been proven
unnecessary under every practical condition.
When the paper identifies a derived dependency, it does not mean the dependency is presently optional.
It may be extremely consequential inside the current architecture.
When the paper says a condition is reality-bound, the claim remains subject to deeper decomposition.
The method does not grant its own categories immunity from revision.
The intended discipline is symmetrical: inherited institutions do not receive automatic necessity, and
proposed alternatives do not receive automatic possibility. Both must answer to reality.
