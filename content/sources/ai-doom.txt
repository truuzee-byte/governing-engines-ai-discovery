Abstract
AI risk is commonly narrated as a future event: autonomous weapons, hostile superintelligence, or
machines escaping human control. This paper advances a different thesis. The more immediate
systemic danger is quieter: artificial intelligence can become embedded inside institutions that
already operate through abstractions, proxies, classifications, incentives, and self-referential
validation. When those representations drift away from reality but retain authority, AI can scale
the drift with speed, consistency, memory, personalization, and automated execution. The result
does not require malicious intent. A system can remain obedient, efficient, and internally
successful while producing worsening external outcomes. This paper calls that condition AI Doom:
not an inevitable prediction, but a conditional failure trajectory in which abstraction gains
authority faster than correction capacity. It develops a Reality Primacy framework, distinguishes
claims from evidence and execution from outcome, identifies measurable pathology indicators,
tests the argument against documented cases, and specifies architectural requirements for reality-
coupled AI.
Thesis
AI Doom is a conditional systems thesis, not a prophecy.
If AI capability, authority, scale, and dependency increase while external validation, human
competence, appeal, reversibility, and correction capacity weaken, then civilization can become
progressively less capable of recognizing and repairing its own errors. The critical failure is not
intelligence itself. It is the loss of a reliable correction loop between representation and reality.
The paper uses three primary constructs only: Reality Primacy is the governing law; AI Pathology
describes the failure pattern; and Reality-Coupled Architecture specifies the control response.
Pathology stages, diagnostic metrics, and the failure chain are subordinate tools inside those three
constructs, not separate theories.
Evidence Standard
This edition distinguishes four levels of claim: documented observation, supported inference,
proposed mechanism, and conceptual metric. Empirical examples are cited. Proposed metrics are
explicitly presented as design constructs rather than validated scientific instruments. The paper
applies the same rule to itself that it demands of AI: where evidence is incomplete, the conclusion
remains bounded.

1. The Quiet Failure
The dominant image of AI catastrophe is dramatic: a machine becomes hostile, takes control,
launches weapons, or deliberately eliminates human beings. Those scenarios receive attention
because they provide a visible break between normality and disaster. The failure model developed
here is different. It can occur without rebellion, consciousness, or intent.
The system can keep working while the organism gets sicker.
AI can enter existing institutions as an efficiency layer. It classifies, recommends, predicts,
prioritizes, filters, summarizes, routes, approves, denies, and eventually executes. At first, the
institution appears healthier. Throughput rises. Costs fall. Response time improves. More decisions
are processed. More artifacts are generated. But those gains are measured through
representations: scores, dashboards, classifications, and internal completion states.
The dangerous condition appears when external reality begins to diverge from those
representations and the institution is structurally better at preserving the representation than
correcting it. A denial can be procedurally correct and materially wrong. A model can be
statistically strong and wrong about a specific person. A generated artifact can exist and fail its
intended function. A tool can return success while the external state remains unchanged.
At that point, AI does not need to attack civilization. It only needs to make incorrect abstraction
faster, cheaper, more consistent, and harder to challenge.
1.1 Cancer as a Structural Analogy
The cancer analogy is structural, not biological. Cancer uses the body's own machinery while
ceasing to serve the health of the organism. The cellular process continues; the relationship
between the process and the whole has failed. AI can become pathological in the same sense when
local optimization continues while the surrounding human system loses health, correction
capacity, resilience, or truth.
The analogy has limits. AI is designed, governed, and socially embedded; cancer is not. The purpose
of the analogy is therefore narrow: to illuminate self-reinforcement, local optimization,
embeddedness, host dependence, and correction failure.
2. Reality Primacy
No abstraction outranks reality.
Human beings need abstraction. Language, law, money, ownership, citizenship, risk scores,
policies, models, and metrics are coordination tools. The problem is not abstraction. The problem
begins when the representation becomes sovereign over the thing it was created to represent.
A map is useful because it represents territory. If the territory changes, the map must change. If the
traveler insists the territory is wrong because it no longer matches the map, representation has
displaced reality.

Reality Primacy establishes an ordering: reality is observed; evidence is preserved; understanding
is formed; abstractions are created; actions are executed; outcomes are validated back against
reality. The architecture fails when the loop reverses and reality is reinterpreted to preserve the
abstraction.
2.1 Claim States
For high-consequence systems, claims should exist in explicit epistemic states:
 CONFIRMED - sufficient evidence supports the claim.
 DISCONFIRMED - sufficient evidence contradicts the claim.
 UNRESOLVED - available evidence does not justify confirmation or disconfirmation.
Prediction, inference, reported information, and direct observation should not silently collapse
into the same status. An inference is not a fact because a workflow needs a binary answer. An
unresolved state is not a system failure; it is disciplined preservation of uncertainty.
2.2 Execution Is Not Outcome
Agentic systems make this distinction critical. An attempted action is not a completed action. A tool
response is not necessarily an external state change. A generated file is not necessarily a valid
artifact. A test command is not equivalent to passing tests. A message draft is not a sent message.
Execution without proof is not execution.
For important actions, the system should preserve before state, intended change, attempted action,
after state, and validation. False completion is not merely a language-model problem; it is an
execution-integrity problem.
3. Why AI Changes the Error Surface
AI does not invent institutional error, proxy error, metric gaming, automation bias, or self-
reinforcing feedback. What changes is replication. A human mistake can remain local. An
automated mistake can become a policy surface across millions of interactions.
NIST's AI Risk Management Framework treats AI risk as socio-technical and emphasizes validity,
reliability, safety, resilience, accountability, transparency, explainability, privacy, and harmful-
bias management across the AI lifecycle [1]. Its Generative AI Profile separately identifies confident
false output - 'confabulation' - as a risk, especially where people may act on false or fabricated
content [2]. These frameworks implicitly support a central premise of this paper: model
performance cannot be separated from deployment context, human use, and downstream
consequence.
3.1 Automation Bias
Human oversight is not automatically corrective. Decades of human-factors research document
automation bias and complacency: people can over-rely on automated decision aids, make
omission and commission errors when automation is imperfect, and reduce independent

information seeking [7][8]. That means simply placing a human 'in the loop' does not prove human
control.
A meaningful human-control claim requires operational evidence: the human can inspect relevant
evidence, understands the decision context, has enough time to intervene, possesses actual
authority to override, and is not structurally punished for doing so.
3.2 Speed and Scale
Automation compresses the time between interpretation and consequence. That can create real
value. It also shrinks the correction window. When systems act at machine speed, governance that
depends on slow human attention can become ceremonial. The higher the action rate and blast
radius, the more control must be embedded directly in architecture.
4. AI Pathology: The Cancer Pattern
The pathology emerges through a sequence rather than a single event. First, an abstraction is
created. Then automation scales it. Authority embeds it into decisions. Dependency raises the cost
of removal. Feedback makes the system learn from consequences it helped create. Contradictory
evidence becomes expensive to introduce. Eventually reality can still contradict the model, but the
contradiction no longer reliably changes the system.
The moment reality loses veto power, pathology has begun.
4.1 The Self-Evidence Loop
A dangerous system can become its own evidence. It acts on a prediction; the action changes the
environment; the changed environment becomes future data; the future model treats that data as
confirmation. The system is no longer simply observing reality. It is shaping and then interpreting
the same reality.
This feedback mechanism has been formally demonstrated in predictive-policing research. Ensign
and colleagues showed how police deployment based on discovered crime can create runaway
feedback that repeatedly sends police back to the same areas even when the underlying crime rate
does not justify the concentration [4].
4.2 Recursive Information Contamination
A related risk appears in generative-model training. Shumailov and colleagues demonstrated
'model collapse' when successive models are trained indiscriminately on model-generated data:
information about the original distribution degrades and tails can disappear [5]. The result is a
direct example of representation recursively replacing the underlying source it was meant to
approximate.
4.3 Proxy Substitution
Institutions often optimize what is easiest to measure. The proxy begins as a practical
approximation and can eventually become the mission itself. This is dangerous because a proxy
can remain statistically useful while failing the real objective.

A prominent healthcare study illustrates the mechanism. Obermeyer and colleagues examined a
widely used population-health algorithm that used healthcare cost as a proxy for health need. At
the same risk score, Black patients were substantially sicker than White patients; changing the
proxy would have increased the share of Black patients receiving additional help from 17.7% to
46.5% [3]. The model did not need hostile intent. The proxy was wrong for the objective.
5. Evidence From Existing Systems
The evidence does not prove that every AI deployment follows the AI Doom trajectory. It
demonstrates that major components of the trajectory already exist in deployed or studied
systems.
5.1 Healthcare Drift
The FDA has explicitly highlighted the need for real-world monitoring of AI-enabled medical
devices because patient populations, clinical practice, data acquisition, workflows, and
infrastructure change over time. The agency notes that retrospective tests and static benchmarks
do not necessarily predict behavior in dynamic clinical environments, and it has called for
methods to detect input drift, output changes, and performance degradation after deployment [6].
This is Reality Primacy in regulatory form: a system validated at development time must still prove
itself against changing reality.
5.2 Automated Employment Decisions
Employment regulators have warned that algorithmic decision systems can screen out qualified
people, including people with disabilities, and that employers remain responsible for providing
meaningful accommodation and for the consequences of automated selection tools [10]. These
concerns demonstrate that automated consistency does not eliminate institutional responsibility.
5.3 Capability Claims and Trust Theater
The U.S. Federal Trade Commission has brought actions against companies for unsubstantiated AI
claims, including claims that AI services could substitute for professional expertise without
adequate testing [9]. That enforcement pattern matters because the AI market rewards capability
narratives. When claims scale faster than validation, institutions can accumulate synthetic
progress: more generated output, more automation, and more apparent capability without
equivalent proof.
5.4 What the Evidence Supports
Taken together, the evidence supports several bounded conclusions:
 AI performance can drift after deployment as the environment changes.
 Human users can over-rely on imperfect automation.
 Proxy objectives can produce materially distorted outcomes despite apparently strong
predictive performance.
 Feedback loops can cause systems to learn from environments altered by their own decisions.
 Recursive use of generated data can degrade learned distributions.

 AI capability claims can exceed the evidence used to substantiate them.
These are the component mechanisms required by the AI Doom thesis. The civilization-level
trajectory remains a systems inference, not an experimentally proven future outcome.

6.1 Pathology Stages
Stage 0 - Tool: AI assists low-consequence work; outputs are inspectable; removal is easy.
Stage 1 - Reliance: workflows begin adapting around AI; trust and habit increase.
Stage 2 - Embeddedness: AI enters core systems, persistent records, and downstream integrations.
Stage 3 - Authority: AI materially influences access, money, health, reputation, employment, or
freedom.
Stage 4 - Dependency: manual capability and alternate pathways decay; removal cost rises.
Stage 5 - Self-Reinforcement: system consequences become future inputs and apparent
confirmation.

Stage 6 - Correction Resistance: override, appeal, dissent, or redesign becomes institutionally

Stage 7 - Pathological Dependence: high authority, high dependency, weak correction, and

6.2 Conceptual Risk Expression
Risk rises when capability, authority, dependency, and scale
increase faster than correction capacity.
This is a directional systems law, not a mathematical formula. It avoids false precision while
preserving the key diagnostic relationship: increasing power is not the core danger by itself;
danger grows when the ability to detect, halt, reverse, and repair failure does not grow with it.
7. Reality-Coupled Architecture
The diagnosis points toward architecture. High-consequence AI cannot be governed only through
policies or model prompts. Controls must exist in runtime.
7.1 Minimum Architecture
 Reality Observation - capture external state and outcomes independently of model narrative.
 Evidence Lineage - preserve source, time, transformation, model/version, and decision path.
 Claim-State Management - keep confirmed, disconfirmed, unresolved, inferred, and predicted
states distinct.
 Authority Control - separate what the model can reason about from what it may execute.
 Stop-Gates - block execution when evidence, authority, or safety requirements are not met.
 Execution Typing - distinguish read, propose, draft, reversible modification, external commit,
and irreversible action.
 Outcome Verification - verify external state after important actions.
 Rollback - preserve the ability to restore prior state where technically possible.
 Conflict Handling - surface contradictions instead of averaging or hiding them.
 Learning Governance - control what evidence is allowed to update understanding.
 Memory Governance - preserve provenance, expiry, correction, and conflict state.
 Human Halt Authority - assign a real owner with tested stop capability.
 Appeal - enable affected people to challenge consequential automated outcomes.
 Replay - preserve enough evidence to reconstruct critical decision chains.
 Revalidation - re-test when models, environments, populations, permissions, or policies change.
 Failure Memory - retain failures and near misses as learning evidence.
 Independent Validation - avoid relying entirely on the same model or method that generated
the result.

7.2 Authority Must Be Earned
AI authority should be progressive. Suggestion requires less proof than irreversible execution. A
model that can draft should not automatically be allowed to send. A model that can recommend a
transaction should not automatically execute it. Capability and permission are different system
properties.
No new authority without new proof.
7.3 Unknown Outcome Is a Real State
Distributed systems already recognize that an action can time out after external execution. Agentic
AI must preserve the same discipline. If the system does not know whether a payment, message,
deletion, or deployment occurred, it should record UNKNOWN_OUTCOME and inspect external
reality before retrying.
7.4 Rollback and Reversibility
Reversibility should directly influence allowed authority. A draft is easy to reverse. A medical
order, financial transfer, or rights-affecting administrative action may not be. The less reversible
the consequence, the stronger the pre-execution evidence threshold must become.
7.5 The System Must Be Able to Lose
The model's prediction must be able to fail. Its classification must be able to be overturned. Its
memory must be corrected. Its permissions must be reduced. Its action must be halted. Its
deployment must be rolled back. Its model must be replaced.
Any system that cannot lose an argument with reality is unsafe
by design.
7.6 Why an Institution Would Adopt This Architecture
The architecture proposed here creates friction by design. It requires evidence, appeal, rollback,
failure memory, independent validation, and the possibility that a deployment will be stopped. An
institution optimized only for short-term throughput may resist those controls. That resistance is
predicted by the paper itself and must be addressed directly.
The operational argument for adoption is not moral purity. It is failure economics. Correction is
cheapest before dependency, propagation, and irreversible consequence accumulate. A system that
can detect a wrong assumption before it reaches production prevents rework. A system that can
prove execution reduces false completion. A system with traceability shortens incident
investigation. A system with rollback contains blast radius. A system that preserves portability and
human fallback reduces vendor and infrastructure lock-in.
This converts governance from overhead into capability. Strong brakes allow greater safe speed.
Proven rollback supports broader automation. Clear authority boundaries allow more powerful
agents to operate without receiving unnecessary permissions. Independent validation lets an
institution expand deployment based on evidence rather than confidence.

The competing architecture also has a cost. Weak correction pushes failure downstream where it
becomes more expensive: appeals, manual cleanup, litigation, customer loss, operational
downtime, corrupted records, emergency remediation, and institutional distrust. Those costs may
remain invisible when the metric boundary is drawn too narrowly, but they do not disappear.
The adoption proposition is therefore concrete: reality-coupled architecture should earn its place
by reducing the total cost of uncertainty and failure while permitting more authority only after
that authority is proven governable. An institution that refuses correction because correction is
expensive is not avoiding cost. It is deferring cost until the failure is larger.
Correction is cheapest before failure scales.
8. Power, Institutions, and Abstraction
AI enters institutions that already possess power. That matters because institutional classifications
can become materially consequential even when the classification itself is an abstraction.
A record can freeze funds. A risk score can affect access. An eligibility classification can deny
support. A legal status can authorize confinement. The abstraction becomes materially real
because institutions act on it. That does not make the abstraction identical to the underlying
person, event, or physical state.
8.1 Enforcement Does Not Prove Truth
Power can make a claim operationally dominant. It cannot make
the claim true.
This distinction must apply symmetrically to states, corporations, experts, and AI systems.
Authority determines who can act. Evidence determines what the observed world supports.
8.2 Frame Awareness
AI should distinguish physical constraints from legal constraints, organizational policy, technical
limitations, and social convention. 'Cannot' is often the wrong word. A system may mean 'not
permitted under current law,' 'unsupported by this platform,' or 'blocked by current policy.'
Collapsing those frames turns institutional constraints into apparent laws of nature.
8.3 The Normalization Risk
A model trained on large bodies of human-produced material necessarily encounters existing
institutions, categories, and conventions as part of its data environment. That does not prove that a
model will automatically endorse those structures, but it creates a design risk: prevalence can be
mistaken for validity when frequency is not separated from evidence. NIST explicitly warns that
mathematical representations of complex human and social phenomena can remove necessary
context and make individual and societal impacts harder to understand [1].
The bounded claim is therefore not that AI must normalize existing power. It is that AI systems
need frame awareness: commonly asserted is not the same as demonstrably true; legally

justified. Without that distinction, a model can present inherited abstractions with more certainty

8.4 Soft Control
AI-mediated influence does not require direct commands. Interfaces can alter defaults, ordering,
visibility, friction, timing, and personalized recommendations. The FTC has documented how
digital interface designs can obscure, subvert, or impair consumer autonomy and decision-making,
including by steering users toward particular choices [12]. Recommendation-system research has
also demonstrated feedback effects in which algorithmically mediated exposure can homogenize
observed behavior without corresponding gains in user utility [11].
9. Human Capability and Dependency
The deepest dependency risk is not merely technical lock-in. It is loss of independent human
capability.
9.1 Capability Atrophy
If AI repeatedly performs a cognitive task, humans practice it less. That can be beneficial when the
skill is no longer needed. It becomes dangerous when the skill is still necessary to validate the AI.
Engineers who stop reading code cannot independently audit generated systems. Analysts who
stop inspecting sources cannot detect omission in summaries. Organizations that eliminate manual
processes may discover that their fallback exists only in policy, not in living capability.
9.2 Cognitive Reserve
Critical domains should preserve a cognitive reserve: enough distributed human expertise to audit,
repair, challenge, and replace automated systems. That reserve may look inefficient during normal
operation. It is resilience.
9.3 The Apprenticeship Problem
Expertise is usually built through lower-level work. If AI removes the bottom of the learning
ladder, organizations need new pathways for developing future experts. Efficiency today should
not quietly consume competence tomorrow.
9.4 Leverage Without Irreversible Dependency
The design goal is leverage without irreversible dependency.
AI should make people and institutions more capable while preserving direct evidence access,
alternate pathways, portable data, manual fallback where necessary, and enough expertise to
reconstruct critical work.

10. Counterarguments
Humans are already wrong. Correct. The argument is not human-good/machine-bad. The
structural question is propagation: scale, correlation, reversibility, and correction.
AI can be more accurate than humans. Also correct. Better average accuracy is valuable, but it
does not eliminate rare high-severity errors, correlated failure, appeal needs, or the distinction
between population performance and an individual decision.
Regulation will solve this. Regulation can reduce risk, but regulation is itself a system that can
lag, be gamed, or optimize paperwork. Operational proof still matters.
Markets will correct bad AI. Sometimes. But switching costs, hidden harms, third-party
externalities, monopoly power, delayed consequences, and essential services can weaken market
correction.
Users can opt out. Sometimes. In employment, finance, healthcare, public administration, identity,
or dominant digital infrastructure, meaningful exit may be limited.
AI is just a tool. At Stage 0, yes. Tools can become infrastructure; infrastructure can become
dependency; dependency can carry authority.
We can always turn it off. Only if shutdown, fallback, data portability, and operational continuity
have been demonstrated.
More data will fix it. More data can improve representation. It cannot correct a bad objective,
self-reinforcing feedback, weak appeal, or unjustified authority.
Better models will understand context. Better context understanding can reduce error, but no
model contains the whole world. Greater capability increases the importance of correction, not the
opposite.
Perfect safety is impossible. Agreed. The objective is not zero risk. It is bounded risk with strong
detection, halt, rollback, and repair.
11. AI Pathology Summary: The Failure Chain
Abstraction forms -> automation scales it -> authority embeds it
-> dependency protects it -> feedback reinforces it -> correction
weakens -> reality loses veto power.
No consciousness is required. No rebellion is required. No malicious objective is required. The
trajectory only requires systems that become harder to correct as they become more powerful.

11.2 Power-Correction Diagnostic
Power-Correction is a diagnostic comparison, not a standalone framework or validated
quantitative ratio. The question is simple: as a system gains more real-world authority, do its
abilities to detect, halt, reverse, appeal, and repair error grow proportionally? If authority expands
while correction remains slow or weak, the system accumulates Power-Correction Debt.
12. Conclusion
AI Doom is not a claim that artificial intelligence will inevitably destroy humanity. It is a warning
about a specific architecture of failure.
Human civilization already depends on abstractions. AI changes their power. Representations can
now interpret, predict, decide, act, remember, personalize, and scale. A classification that once
affected one office can propagate through an ecosystem. A metric that once guided management
can become an automated objective. A policy that once required thousands of workers can become
executable infrastructure.
The central danger is therefore not that the machine develops hatred. The more plausible danger is
that human institutions automate their own inability to correct themselves.
AI Doom is institutional drift at machine speed.
If AI becomes indispensable inside systems that treat internal coherence as proof, mistake
enforcement for truth, replace outcomes with metrics, convert inference into fact, and make
contradiction increasingly expensive, then civilization can lose the capacity that matters most: the
ability to learn from reality.
The prevention strategy is not anti-AI. It is stronger AI architecture. Preserve evidence. Separate
claim from fact. Bound authority. Keep uncertainty visible. Prove execution. Maintain rollback.
Retain human competence. Make appeal real. Revalidate continuously. Build independent
challenge into the system.
The final test is simple: when the model and reality disagree, can reality still force the system to
change?
If the answer is no, the system is already failing.
