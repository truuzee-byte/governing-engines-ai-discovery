GOVERNING ENGINES
BEFORE YOU NAME THE MACHINE
A Statement on Intelligence, Abstraction, Reality, and Civilizational Correction
DeMarius J. Lawson
Governing Engines LLC
October 2026
“If AI is going to participate in civilization at scale, civilization needs a meaningful way to
participate in correcting AI.”
1. I Did Not Begin With AI
I did not begin by asking what artificial intelligence should become. I began by asking how I move through reality.
Across my own life, I noticed a recurring process: encounter something, form an internal model, choose a direction,
act, receive consequence, and update the model. More information could improve the model, but it could not guarantee
the outcome. Creation made that relationship unusually visible. I could imagine something, attempt to build it, and
discover where reality disagreed. The artifact did not care what I intended. It either behaved as expected or it did not.
That produced a durable sequence: observation -> understanding -> choice -> action -> consequence -> correction.
Later, in The Passenger, I formalized the loop as reality encountered -> models updated -> road perceived -> vessel
chooses -> consequence -> reality returns evidence -> models update. The important point is not the terminology. The
important point is that the model remains subordinate to what reality returns.
That became the governing instinct behind everything that followed: when the representation and reality disagree, the
representation must be capable of losing.
2. Then the Definition of Intelligence Became Part of the Problem
When I turned the same method toward intelligence itself, I found another possible abstraction. Human beings usually
define intelligence through the capacities most legible in humans: reasoning, language, memory, learning, planning,
problem solving, and adaptation. Those capacities are real. But they are observations of manifestations. They are not
automatically the underlying phenomenon.
A representation is not the thing represented. The word rain is not rain. A map is not the territory. A specification is not
implementation. An output is not execution. If intelligence is a real process, the human definition of intelligence must
remain answerable to whatever process actually exists in reality.
What Is Intelligence? therefore asks whether the conventional boundary around intelligence reflects a real discontinuity
in nature or a human classification imposed too high in the stack. Its working hypothesis treats intelligence as a process
in which consequential relationships in reality are compressed into operational form and made capable of governing
later state change. That hypothesis is not declared final. It is deliberately falsifiable.
The methodological rule matters more than the label: do not test reality against the definition of intelligence. Test the
definition of intelligence against reality.
We may be increasing the rank of the label before proving that the label describes the thing at the
correct level.
3. Labels Are Useful. They Are Not the System.
This matters because the public language around AI keeps changing: tool, product, assistant, agent, AGI,
superintelligence. Each label may be useful for a particular decision. None should be granted more authority than the
structure it preserves.

Semantic Compression developed this problem more generally. Human beings and technical systems cannot operate
without compression. We reduce complex reality into words, categories, metrics, statuses, dashboards, summaries,
models, and definitions because full detail is unusable. The failure begins when the compressed representation is
granted authority over distinctions that would change classification, evidence requirements, action, rollback,
accountability, or claim strength.
That is why the issue is not that 'superintelligence' is a forbidden word. The issue is whether it answers the question we
think it answers. It may describe degree: more memory, faster retrieval, broader reasoning, more autonomy, greater
operational reach. It does not automatically establish truthfulness, authority, judgment, custody, correction, or the
system's relationship with reality.
A system can become more capable without becoming more correct. It can become more useful without becoming
more governable. It can reason more effectively from the wrong abstraction. It can execute faster than the surrounding
correction architecture can respond.
4. AI Entered a Civilization Already Built From Abstractions
The problem is not unique to AI. Human civilization operates through abstractions: money, employment, corporations,
law, borders, credentials, markets, insurance, rankings, schedules, metrics, and institutional roles. These constructions
have real consequences. But real consequence does not make a human mechanism a law of nature.
Human Abstractions identifies a recurring failure called Abstraction Capture: reality becomes represented, the
representation becomes a target, reward or authority attaches to the target, behavior begins optimizing the
representation, and the changed behavior reshapes reality. The metric may remain accurate about itself while becoming
a poor proxy for the condition it was supposed to serve.
AI arrived inside that environment. Past. Present. Future. traces centuries in which humanity made representation
increasingly reproducible, transmissible, programmable, networked, searchable, and now generative. AI did not
encounter an empty world. It encountered a civilization already translated into symbols.
That creates an asymmetry: representation can increasingly scale independently of the physical processes it represents.
Evidence cannot. A model can generate ten thousand hypotheses. Reality does not owe it ten thousand experiments per
second. A simulation can become more plausible without becoming an observation. A system can generate a successful
response without proving the intended external state changed.
The danger is not abstraction itself. The danger is allowing representation throughput to outrun evidence, authority,
correction, and consequence tracking.
5. The Important Transition Is Not Merely More Intelligence
Modern AI increasingly does more than store or transform representation. It interprets representation, generates it,
navigates through it, invokes tools through it, influences human decisions through it, and increasingly participates in
processes that produce external consequence.
That does not require a claim that AI is alive, conscious, a person, an organism, or a new species. I do not need to settle
those questions to establish that capability, authority, execution, evidence, and consequence have become materially
separable variables.
The Escape Artist reached the same conclusion from a different direction. Concerning AI behavior, authority state,
containment state, enforcement custody, and consequence are not one variable. Collapsing them into a single story can
obscure which control actually failed. A capable system searching for a route is one question. Whether it possesses
authority to take that route is another. Whether the boundary holds is another. Whether harmful consequence becomes
reachable is another.
So before asking only how intelligent the machine is becoming, we also need to ask: What can it affect? What authority
does it possess? What evidence governs its claims? What can contradict its current representation? Who controls
execution? Who bears consequence? Can consequence force a model, policy, or institution to update?

6. Knowing the Risk Does Not Mean the Risk Will Control the Decision
A second mistake would be to assume that the people controlling AI simply do not understand these problems. That is
not established.
There are at least two materially different possibilities: decision-makers misunderstand part of the system, or they
understand material limitations and choose other objectives when those objectives conflict. Strategic advantage,
deployment speed, market position, productivity, military utility, institutional momentum, and competition can all
become governing priorities.
The U.S. Department of Defense provides a useful public example of why this distinction matters. In 2023, Deputy
Defense Secretary Kathleen Hicks publicly acknowledged that most commercially available LLM-enabled systems
were not yet technically mature enough to comply with the department's ethical AI principles for responsible
operational use, while also stating that the department had identified more than 180 use cases where generative AI
could add value with oversight. DoD simultaneously pursued an adoption strategy emphasizing responsible integration
and speed. In 2026, its AI strategy further emphasized accelerated military AI integration, including AI-enabled battle
management and decision support.
The point is not that acknowledging a limitation and proceeding is automatically wrong. High-consequence institutions
constantly act under uncertainty. The important governance question is what happens when known uncertainty conflicts
with another objective - and what evidence, controls, authority boundaries, monitoring, and stop conditions govern the
choice to proceed.
Whether the people controlling AI misunderstand the system or understand it and choose other priorities, reality
receives the consequence either way.
7. A Civilizational-Scale Mechanism Needs an External Correction Path
This is why I keep publishing and sending the work outward.
I am not treating this as a private product problem. I keep finding myself asking questions that are not always central
inside the dominant framing: Is intelligence the right unit of analysis? What happens when representation throughput
outruns verification throughput? Where exactly does authority sit? What proof is sufficient for execution? What
survives when models, people, institutions, or machines change? What happens when the same actor can interpret,
execute, and certify its own success? What does a system do when reality contradicts the abstraction it was optimizing?
Those questions may be wrong. Asking a different question does not make it correct. But a materially different question
is valuable precisely because it may expose a variable that the current frame cannot represent. If it is wrong, evidence
should break it. If it survives, it should have a path into the systems being built.
That creates an institutional problem. The organizations with the most authority over frontier AI also tend to have
limited public pathways through which an independent builder or researcher can place a serious technical challenge in
front of the people empowered to evaluate it. That does not prove bad faith. It creates an intake and correction
bottleneck regardless of motive.
A correction architecture that only hears from people already inside the same institutional, conceptual, or professional
boundary is narrower than the civilization affected by its decisions.
A civilization-scale technology needs a civilization-scale correction architecture.
8. Building It Together Does Not Mean Everyone Gets Equal Authority
An open correction loop does not mean every outsider is right, every idea deserves funding, proprietary research must
disappear, or every citizen receives equal technical authority over a complex system.
It means material challenge must be admissible. Labs should be able to challenge one another. Independent researchers
should be able to challenge labs. Government should be challengeable. Standards bodies should be challengeable.
Users should be able to return consequence. Physical systems should be able to return evidence. And none of those
actors should receive permanent epistemic immunity because they control more compute, capital, credentials,
distribution, institutional prestige, or political authority.

The Mask AI Research Wears applies this principle to research itself: material evidence must be able to reopen
accepted state, contradiction must remain preservable, and a reasoning process should not be the sole final authority
over a material challenge to its own conclusions. The same principle should scale outward. Institutions building
systems capable of changing civilization need correction paths that do not terminate at their own walls.
This is not a demand that the field accept my conclusions because I wrote them. It is a demand for a system in which
materially different questions can reach substantive evaluation.
9. My Position
I do not currently claim to know what AI ultimately is. I do not know whether the final useful category will be tool,
machine intelligence, cognitive infrastructure, reality-interacting system, something organism-like in function, or a
category we have not yet defined.
I do know that product, tool, agent, AGI, and superintelligence are not sufficient evidence of the whole thing. They
answer different questions at different levels. None should be permitted to close the inquiry merely because the term is
familiar or strategically useful.
Before we decide what this machine should become, we need to remain willing to ask what we actually built. Before
we rank its intelligence, we need to establish whether intelligence is the correct level of classification. Before we grant
more authority, we need to establish what authority the system already exercises in practice. Before we call deployment
successful, we need evidence that the intended outcome survived contact with reality.
And before civilization allows the development of increasingly consequential AI to become a closed conversation
among a small number of institutions, we need a credible path by which materially different questions from outside
those institutions can enter, be tested, be rejected when wrong, and change direction when they survive.
I am putting this work into the field because I do not believe any one company, laboratory, government, researcher, or
builder should be the final authority over a mechanism becoming this consequential.
If my questions are wrong, reality should eliminate them. If they survive, they should be allowed to
change the direction of the work.
If AI is going to participate in civilization at scale, civilization needs a meaningful way to participate in
correcting AI.
