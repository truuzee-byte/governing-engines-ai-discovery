ABSTRACT
Artificial intelligence is increasingly justified through claims of productivity, speed, automation, and
lower production cost. Many of those claims may be true. But the economic boundary at which they are
measured is often narrower than the outcome being purchased.
A system can make one operation faster without making the complete objective cheaper. It can reduce
generation cost while increasing review, correction, integration, validation, coordination, recovery, or
expert-supervision cost. It can make an individual worker faster without producing proportional
enterprise value.
This paper proposes that AI economics should therefore be evaluated at the boundary of the valid
outcome, not merely at the boundary of the AI operation. It introduces Total Cost to Valid Outcome
(TCVO) as a proposed measurement framework for distinguishing cost elimination, cost reduction, and
cost migration across AI-assisted production systems.
The paper does not argue that AI fails to reduce cost. It argues that the reduction must be measured at
the correct boundary, under materially equivalent outcome requirements, with economically
necessary human and machine work included in the record.
GOVERNING RULE
Measure AI economics at the boundary of the outcome being purchased.
EXECUTIVE SUMMARY
The economic case for AI is often expressed through local metrics: time saved per task, cost per
generation, tokens consumed, model calls, headcount avoided, or throughput gained. These metrics are
useful, but they do not automatically establish the total cost of accomplishing the objective.
 Task efficiency and system efficiency are different properties.
 An AI output is not automatically a valid outcome.
 Human labor can be eliminated, reduced, relocated, or transformed rather than simply removed.
 Failure paths, retries, correction, integration, validation, and recovery can materially alter
economics.
 Productivity gains at the individual level do not automatically propagate into enterprise value.
 Governance and validation are costs, but they may also prevent larger downstream costs.
 A fair comparison must hold the destination constant while allowing the pathway to change.
The proposed economic unit is the valid outcome: an outcome that satisfies the requirements necessary
for the objective to count as successfully reached. The proposed metric is Total Cost to Valid Outcome
(TCVO): the total attributable human, machine, operational, validation, and failure-path cost required
to reach that valid outcome.
1. THE CLAIM IS BIGGER THAN THE METRIC
One of the dominant economic narratives around artificial intelligence is straightforward: human
expertise plus AI produces more output, faster, at lower cost. There is substantial reason to take that
possibility seriously.

AI systems can draft text, generate software, create images and video, summarize documents, analyze
data, retrieve information, automate workflow stages, and perform tasks that previously required
substantially more human effort. Meta, for example, describes a future centered on expanded
individual capability and invention rather than automation alone, and gives concrete examples of
creation becoming dramatically faster. [2]
That is evidence of a capability shift. It is not, by itself, a complete economic model.
A cheaper generation does not necessarily mean a cheaper production. A faster task does not
necessarily mean a faster workflow. A more productive individual does not necessarily mean a more
productive organization. And a lower-cost operation does not necessarily mean a lower-cost outcome.
McKinsey's 2026 research makes part of this distinction explicitly. Its survey of 750 employees and
leaders found that most organizations remained early in their AI transformation journeys; only 11
percent of surveyed leaders placed their organizations in the reinvention horizon, and the majority
across all three horizons reported that AI had yet to deliver meaningful enterprise value across
business performance, cost savings, employee experience, or customer outcomes. [1]
The authors further argue that individual productivity gains rarely translate into lasting advantage
when the organization around them remains unchanged. [1]
MEASUREMENT QUESTION
At what point in the system should AI efficiency be measured?
2. LOCAL EFFICIENCY IS NOT SYSTEM EFFICIENCY
Consider a simplified production chain:
OBJECTIVE  ->  WORK  ->  OUTPUT  ->  REVIEW  ->  CORRECTION  ->
INTEGRATION  ->  VALIDATION  ->  ACCEPTED OUTCOME
An AI system may substantially reduce the cost of Work -> Output. That matters. But the economic
claim frequently being made is larger: the objective itself became cheaper to accomplish.
Those statements are not equivalent.
Suppose a professional task historically required four hours and produced a usable result. AI reduces
generation time to twenty minutes. Measured at the generation boundary, the productivity
improvement is enormous. But suppose the resulting artifact then requires review, factual correction,
regeneration, reconciliation of inconsistencies, integration into an existing workflow, quality
assurance, and final professional approval.
The AI may still have created substantial savings. Or it may not have. We cannot know from the
twenty-minute generation number. That number measures a stage. The economic claim concerns the
system.

3. OUTPUT COST IS NOT OUTCOME COST
An output is something a system produced. A valid outcome is something that satisfies the conditions
required for the objective to be considered successfully reached.
 A generated legal memo is an output. A legally defensible memo suitable for its intended use is an
outcome.
 Generated software is an output. Software that satisfies requirements, integrates, and survives
required testing is an outcome.
 An AI-generated shot is an output. A shot satisfying continuity, timing, quality, technical, and
acceptance requirements is an outcome.
 A customer-service answer is an output. Correct resolution of the customer's problem may be the
outcome.
The distinction appears in verification-oriented engineering as well. The DASE Capability Console
explicitly rejects plausible-looking numerical output as sufficient evidence, instead using independently
available reference values, independent computational routes, and deliberately invalid cases to
determine whether computed results are actually correct. [5]
This is fundamentally a boundary question: where does successful production end? If success is
declared when the model generates something, generation cost may be sufficient. If success is declared
when the generated artifact survives independent requirements, the accounting boundary must
continue through validation.
4. THE HUMAN MAY BE INSIDE THE SYSTEM
Many AI productivity claims involve experienced professionals using AI. This combination can work
extremely well. But experienced professionals often do more than initiate generation.
 recognize bad outputs and missing information
 detect implausible claims or domain errors
 reconstruct intent and restore context
 correct terminology and preserve continuity
 choose among alternatives and reject unusable generations
 integrate outputs into broader work
 determine whether the result is actually safe, valid, or good enough to use
Those functions have economic value. If they are necessary to transform AI output into a valid
outcome, they belong inside the economic system being measured.
The University of Chicago Law School's 2026 AI strategy illustrates one dimension of this issue. While
embracing AI as an increasingly important part of legal practice, the school simultaneously emphasizes
critical thinking, strategic judgment, professional judgment, oral advocacy, and the ability to use AI
responsibly rather than merely produce answers with it. [3]
If expert judgment remains necessary to determine whether an AI-produced artifact is valid, then
expert judgment has not disappeared from the production system merely because generation was
automated. It may have changed position.

5. COST ELIMINATION, COST REDUCTION, AND COST MIGRATION
The most important accounting distinction in AI-assisted production may not be automation versus
non-automation. It may be what actually happened to the cost.
Pattern Definition Illustrative example
COST ELIMINATION A required activity no longer needs to
occur.
A manual transcription step
disappears because the system
produces an accepted structured
record directly.
COST REDUCTION The activity remains, but fewer
resources are required.
Review still occurs, but the reviewer
needs 15 minutes instead of 60.
COST MIGRATION Cost decreases in one part of the
system while appearing elsewhere.
Generation becomes cheap, but
expert correction, integration, or
validation expands.
COST TRANSFORMATION The old activity disappears, but a new
activity becomes necessary because
the architecture changed.
Manual drafting falls while prompt
design, exception handling, or AI-
output governance becomes
necessary.
A local metric can correctly report a large efficiency gain while the system-level reality is cost
migration. The metric is not necessarily false. The boundary is incomplete.
KEY DISTINCTION
The economic question is not only whether labor disappeared from the measured task. It is whether
economically necessary work disappeared from the system.
6. THE FAILURE PATH HAS A COST
Economic analysis frequently centers the successful path. Production systems also contain failure
paths.
OBJECTIVE -> ATTEMPT -> OUTPUT -> EVALUATION -> ACCEPT  OR
CORRECT -> RETRY -> RE-EVALUATE
Every unsuccessful generation may be individually inexpensive. Collectively, however, failed attempts
can consume compute, API spending, operator time, reviewer time, workflow latency, attention,
opportunity cost, integration effort, correction labor, and downstream recovery.
This does not mean retries are inherently waste. Iteration is part of many productive processes. The
relevant question is whether the complete cost of iteration is present in the economic record.

If only the accepted generation is counted, the system appears cheaper than the process that actually
produced it.
7. COST CAN MOVE ACROSS ORGANIZATIONAL BOUNDARIES
AI economics becomes especially difficult when cost is displaced from one organizational boundary
into another.
 A model provider may reduce inference cost while the user organization absorbs greater
verification cost.
 A development tool may reduce code-generation cost while engineering absorbs debugging and
integration cost.
 A creative platform may reduce shot-generation cost while artists or editors absorb continuity
repair.
 A customer-service system may reduce front-line handling time while escalation teams absorb
difficult unresolved cases.
 A procurement system may reduce administrative work while compliance teams absorb additional
review.
Each participant can report a locally accurate number. The complete system can still experience a
different economic result.
This is why a number may be correct while the conclusion drawn from it is too large.
8. THE PROPOSED UNIT: VALID OUTCOME
This paper proposes the valid outcome as a useful economic unit for AI systems.
DEFINITION
VALID OUTCOME: an outcome that satisfies the explicitly defined conditions necessary for the
objective to count as successfully achieved.
Depending on the domain, those conditions may include technical correctness, factual accuracy,
completion requirements, quality thresholds, authority requirements, policy compliance, safety
requirements, integration requirements, continuity requirements, stakeholder acceptance, or other
domain-specific criteria.
For controlled comparisons, the definition should be established before results are evaluated.
Otherwise the success condition can move to fit the output.
This gives us a more useful denominator than tokens, generations, model calls, artifacts, employee
hours saved, or outputs produced. Those remain useful operational metrics. None necessarily
establishes successful objective completion.
9. TOTAL COST TO VALID OUTCOME
PROPOSED METRIC
TCVO = total attributable cost required to produce a valid outcome.

A generalized accounting structure may include:
TCVO = AI COST + HUMAN PRODUCTION + REVIEW + CORRECTION +
RETRY + REWORK + COORDINATION + INTEGRATION + VALIDATION +
RECOVERY + ATTRIBUTABLE FAILURE-PATH COST
Not every category applies to every system, and categories must be defined carefully to avoid double
counting. The purpose is not to maximize the number of cost categories. It is to prevent economically
necessary work from disappearing merely because it occurs outside the AI operation.
For repeated production, a useful derived measure is Cost Per Valid Outcome (CPVO):
CPVO = TOTAL ATTRIBUTABLE EXECUTION COST / NUMBER OF VALID
OUTCOMES
This can be compared between conventional and AI-assisted architectures. The AI-assisted workflow is
economically superior when it reaches materially equivalent required outcomes at lower total cost, or
when superior capability or quality justifies additional cost.
Cheaper is not the only form of economic value. AI may produce greater quality, capability,
throughput, accessibility, responsiveness, or entirely new outputs that conventional systems could not
economically produce. The destination has to remain comparable.
10. SAME DESTINATION, DIFFERENT PATH
A fair productivity comparison requires equivalent destinations, not identical workflows.
Suppose Workflow A produces a carefully reviewed professional artifact. Workflow B produces an AI
draft in one tenth the time. Comparing those numbers is meaningless unless Workflow B is also
required to reach the quality and validity threshold represented by Workflow A.
Conversely, it would be equally unfair to impose unnecessary historical steps on the AI workflow
merely because the conventional system used them.
FAIR-COMPARISON RULE
Preserve objective equivalence, outcome equivalence, and validation equivalence. Allow the
pathway to change.
11. FIVE ECONOMIC RESULTS THE EVIDENCE MAY SHOW
A. STRONG SYSTEM-LEVEL EFFICIENCY
AI reduces both local task cost and total cost to valid outcome.

B. LOCAL EFFICIENCY, SMALLER SYSTEM EFFICIENCY
AI dramatically improves one operation, but part of the gain is consumed by review, correction,
integration, or other downstream work. The system still improves, but less than the headline metric
suggests.
C. COST MIGRATION
AI makes one stage cheaper while moving approximately equivalent cost elsewhere. Little system-level
economic improvement occurs.
D. NEGATIVE SYSTEM ECONOMICS
AI lowers visible production cost but introduces enough error, rework, validation burden, integration
difficulty, or failure cost that the valid outcome becomes more expensive.
E. CAPABILITY EXPANSION
AI does not reduce total cost but produces greater capability, quality, volume, accessibility,
responsiveness, or previously infeasible outcomes. The correct economic conclusion is increased value
rather than reduced cost.
All five are legitimate empirical possibilities. A measurement framework should not decide which one
occurred before the evidence exists.
12. FROM INDIVIDUAL PRODUCTIVITY TO ENTERPRISE VALUE
An employee can perform one activity faster while the organization around that employee continues to
contain the same approval structures, information bottlenecks, dependencies, integration constraints,
legacy systems, authority boundaries, review requirements, coordination delays, and institutional
processes.
McKinsey's 2026 research identifies this gap directly. It argues that organizations further along in AI
transformation redesign workflows and operating models rather than simply inserting AI into existing
tasks. Its broader analysis states that individual productivity with technology is not predictive of
organizational health and sustained performance. [1]
A system-boundary interpretation explains why: productivity realized inside one node does not
automatically propagate through the whole chain. Interfaces, dependencies, and constraints can absorb
the gain.
13. GOVERNANCE IS AN ECONOMIC VARIABLE
Governance is frequently treated only as overhead. Sometimes it is overhead. But governance can also
prevent cost.
Current governance frameworks increasingly emphasize lifecycle controls, validation, monitoring,
accountability, testing, incident reporting, and human oversight because AI operation does not end
when a model produces an answer. UNESCO's 2026 policy brief maps multiple regulatory approaches
for governing AI, while technical governance guides such as ISACA's emphasize lifecycle risk
management, verification and validation, operation and monitoring, and accountability. [6][7]

Kenya's 2026 AI and Emerging Technologies Policy similarly addresses design, development, testing,
deployment, monitoring, and decommissioning, while identifying fragmented governance and
institutional coordination as sources of implementation inefficiency. It also calls for testing protocols,
incident reporting, auditability, accountability, traceability, and meaningful human oversight for
higher-risk systems. [4]
Those activities cost money. Their economic value cannot be established by counting their cost alone.
A validation step costing $10 is economically wasteful if it prevents nothing of value. The same $10 may
be economically beneficial if it reliably prevents $100 of rework or expected loss.
ECONOMIC RULE
Governance cost should be measured against both the burden it introduces and the downstream
expenditure it prevents.
14. MEASUREMENT DISCIPLINE
A useful AI economic study should separate at least three classes of claim:
OBSERVED
Directly measured: execution time, human review time, attempts, API cost, corrections, accepted
outputs, failed outputs.
DERIVED
Calculated from observed evidence: cost per valid outcome, failure rate, first-pass acceptance rate,
correction burden, measured savings percentage.
PROJECTED
Extrapolated beyond the observed sample: annual savings, enterprise-wide workforce effects, industry-
wide impact, million-operation savings.
These categories should not be treated as equivalent. An observed thirty-minute reduction in one task
is evidence about that task. A projection of $100 million in annual savings is an economic model. The
latter may be reasonable, but assumptions entered the chain and should remain visible.
15. AN OUTCOME-BOUNDARY TEST FOR AI ECONOMIC CLAIMS
Before accepting a claim that AI made production cheaper, ask the following in order:
1. What objective was the system actually trying to accomplish?
2. What counted as a valid outcome?
3. Where did the reported cost measurement stop?
4. What human and machine work occurred after that boundary?
5. How many attempts, failures, retries, corrections, and discarded outputs occurred?
6. What integration, coordination, validation, governance, or recovery work was necessary?
7. Was any labor eliminated, or was it reduced, migrated, or transformed?

8. Were the AI and conventional workflows required to reach materially equivalent destinations?
9. Which reported claims are observed, derived, and projected?
10. Does the evidence establish lower total cost to the valid outcome, or only lower local cost inside the
chain?
This test does not presume a negative answer. It forces the economic claim and the measurement
boundary to match.
16. WHAT WOULD FALSIFY THE CONCERN?
The framework should itself remain falsifiable.
The strongest evidence against the concern raised by this paper would be repeated studies
demonstrating that AI-assisted and conventional systems are given materially equivalent objectives;
both are held to materially equivalent outcome and validation standards; economically significant
human and machine work is measured; failure, retry, correction, integration, and validation are
accounted for; and AI-assisted systems consistently produce equal or superior valid outcomes at
materially lower total cost.
If that evidence accumulates, the appropriate conclusion is not resistance. It is that AI genuinely
reduced the economics of production at the system boundary.
REALITY GETS THE LAST VOTE.
17. CONCLUSION
Artificial intelligence may become one of the most economically consequential technologies ever
developed. That makes measurement more important, not less.
The central problem is not that current AI productivity numbers are necessarily false. Many may be
entirely accurate. The problem is that a locally accurate number can be asked to support a system-level
conclusion it was never designed to prove.
AI can make a task faster without making the complete workflow faster. It can make an output cheaper
without making a valid outcome cheaper. It can remove one category of labor while relocating or
transforming another. It can increase individual productivity without producing proportional
organizational value. And it can also genuinely reduce all of those costs at once.
We should be able to tell the difference.
MEASURE AI ECONOMICS AT THE BOUNDARY OF THE OUTCOME BEING
PURCHASED.
If the customer is purchasing a generation, measure generation cost. If the organization is purchasing a
completed task, measure completed-task cost. If the system exists to produce a validated professional
result, measure the complete cost required to reach that validated result. If the objective is enterprise
value, do not stop measuring at individual productivity.
The economic promise of artificial intelligence does not need weaker accounting. If the promise is real,
better accounting should make it easier to see.

CLAIM STATUS
ESTABLISHED: AI can reduce the cost and time of particular operations and tasks in many contexts.
ESTABLISHED: Individual AI adoption and individual productivity do not automatically establish
enterprise-level economic value; McKinsey's 2026 research explicitly distinguishes these levels. [1]
SUPPORTED: Human judgment, review, integration, governance, validation, and workflow redesign
can remain materially relevant after AI is introduced. [1][3][4][7]
PROPOSED: Total Cost to Valid Outcome is a useful system-boundary framework for comparing AI-
assisted and conventional production architectures.
OPEN: Across industries, how frequently do reported AI task-level savings survive complete valid-
outcome accounting?
RESEARCH AGENDA
The framework produces a concrete empirical agenda rather than a rhetorical conclusion. Future
studies should compare conventional and AI-assisted workflows under equivalent destination
requirements and collect complete execution evidence across the successful and unsuccessful paths.
 software development: generation, debugging, integration, testing, review, and production
acceptance
 creative production: generation, continuity, revision, editorial integration, technical acceptance,
and final delivery
 professional services: drafting, research, expert review, correction, client use, and accountability
 customer operations: automated handling, escalation, repeat contact, resolution quality, and
downstream recovery
 enterprise workflows: individual time savings versus end-to-end cycle time, cost, and
organizational value
The key empirical question remains the same across domains: did AI reduce the total cost of reaching
the valid destination, or did the cost change location?

METHOD NOTE
This paper is a conceptual measurement framework informed by publicly available research, policy,
governance, and technical verification materials. It does not claim that all AI deployments exhibit cost
migration, nor does it estimate industry-wide savings or losses. Its purpose is to define a measurement
boundary capable of distinguishing local efficiency from system-level economic performance and to
state the evidence required for stronger conclusions.
