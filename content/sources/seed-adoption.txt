SEED ADOPTION
When Does Technological Displacement Actually Begin?
DeMarius J. Lawson

“The technology that eventually replaces a function rarely enters society
announcing that it intends to replace the function. It enters by being useful.”

ABSTRACT
Technological displacement is usually recognized late. A worker loses a position. An occupation
contracts. A machine performs work that once required a person. Only then does society begin asking
whether technology displaced labor.
But the causal chain often began much earlier. A technology first enters as assistance. It solves a
problem, lowers effort, increases speed, reduces cost, improves safety, or expands capability.
Infrastructure is then constructed around it. Institutions reorganize. Capital is invested. Workers learn
new workflows. Consumers develop expectations. New generations inherit the system. As the
technology improves, functions that once required people can progressively move into the infrastructure
itself.
This paper calls that earlier transition seed adoption.
Displacement potential can begin when society adopts an assistive capability deeply
enough that later substitution can inherit its infrastructure, workflow position,
legitimacy, data, or dependency without requiring society to make an equivalent
first-adoption decision again.
Seed adoption does not mean displacement is inevitable. Technology can create occupations, expand
industries, increase demand, improve working conditions, remove dangerous labor, and transform jobs
without eliminating them. The argument is narrower: early adoption can change the feasible future by
lowering the friction of later substitution.
At what point does assistance become infrastructure for substitution?
1. THE PROBLEM WITH WHERE WE BEGIN COUNTING
We usually locate technological displacement at the visible consequence: a factory reduces headcount,
a warehouse requires fewer people for a given output, software performs administrative work, or a
machine performs a physical task that once belonged exclusively to human labor.
That makes intuitive sense because displacement has finally become visible. But it can hide the lineage
that produced the outcome. Before a worker disappears, a capability may have been accepted,
infrastructure installed, investment accumulated, operating procedures changed, dependency formed,
and the technology improved. Functions can migrate gradually before employment consequences
become obvious.
The final labor consequence may therefore be several generations removed from the original adoption
decision. By the time society asks whether it should accept the technology, the meaningful adoption
decision may already be over.
2. WHAT SEED ADOPTION ADDS TO EXISTING LITERATURE

Seed Adoption is not presented as a replacement for existing work on technological path dependence,
lock-in, task automation, or labor-market adjustment. It is an upstream governance concept built on top
of those literatures.
Paul A. David’s work on QWERTY emphasized how temporally remote events, technical
interrelatedness, economies of scale, and quasi-irreversibility can shape later technological outcomes.
[1] W. Brian Arthur developed a broader account of increasing returns, positive feedback, competing
technologies, lock-in, and path-dependent economic processes. [2]
In labor economics, David Autor has emphasized that automation both substitutes for and complements
labor, while changing the kinds of tasks workers perform. [3] Daron Acemoglu and Pascual Restrepo
formalize a task-based framework in which automation creates a displacement effect while the creation
of new tasks can reinstate labor demand. [4]
Those frameworks answer important questions: How do early choices constrain later options? Why do
systems become locked in? Which tasks move from labor to capital? What new tasks emerge?
Seed Adoption asks a different governance question:
When has an assistive capability become embedded deeply enough that later
substitution can expand without receiving an equivalent new adoption decision?
Path dependence describes how history constrains the future. Lock-in describes persistence and
switching difficulty. Task frameworks describe the allocation of work between labor and capital. Seed
Adoption identifies the governance transition at which later capability generations begin inheriting
legitimacy, infrastructure, workflow access, data, or institutional permission from earlier ones.
Later capability should not inherit the legitimacy of earlier adoption merely because it
uses the same infrastructure.
3. DEFINITION AND QUALIFICATION TEST
Canonical definition. Seed Adoption is the point at which an assistive technology becomes embedded
deeply enough that later capability expansion can inherit its infrastructure, workflow position, data,
institutional legitimacy, distribution, or dependency without requiring an equivalent first-adoption decision
again.
Not every technology qualifies. The concept is intended to be falsifiable rather than universal.
A note on scope before proceeding. This section defines whether a Seed Adoption condition exists at
all — an existence test. It should not be confused with “function compression,” introduced later in
Section 18, which is a severity measure applied only after Seed Adoption has already been found
present. Existence and severity are answered by different tests and can move independently: a system
can clear the existence test in Section 3 while scoring low on the severity scale in Section 18, or the
reverse.

Task displacement, function displacement, role transformation, and worker displacement are related but
not interchangeable. An industry can eliminate tasks while expanding employment. An occupation can
transform without shrinking. A technology can lower labor input per unit of output while total output grows
enough to sustain or increase employment.
5. EFFICIENCY IS NOT AUTOMATICALLY DISPLACEMENT
Autor cautions against treating automation as pure substitution: automation can complement labor, raise
output, increase demand, and alter the composition of jobs rather than simply erase them. [3] Acemoglu
and Restrepo similarly distinguish a displacement effect from a reinstatement effect created by new
tasks. [4]
Technology becomes displacement-relevant when reduced human input per unit of output changes how
much labor is required, what kinds of labor are required, where labor is required, which functions remain
human, or how many workers are needed.
The question is not only whether productivity increased. It is where human necessity
moved.
6. CASE: AUTOMOBILES AND SYSTEM REORGANIZATION
The automobile did not instantly eliminate horse transportation. Cars entered an existing transportation
system and then accumulated manufacturing capacity, roads, fuel distribution, repair networks,
insurance, traffic law, licensing, parking, and new settlement patterns. The Smithsonian’s record of the
American carriage trade notes that carriage building had once been one of the country’s largest and
most dynamic industries, and that the Carriage Builders National Association met for the last time in
1926 as the automobile displaced horse-drawn carriage production. [5]
The important mechanism is not a one-machine-for-one-worker swap. It is system reorganization. Some
functions contracted, others transformed, and entirely new industries became possible. The automobile
therefore demonstrates that displacement and employment creation can occur simultaneously.
Human necessity did not simply disappear. It moved into a different system.

7. CASE: TELEPHONES AND THE FUNCTION INSIDE THE
NETWORK
Early telephone networks required human switchboard operators. As telephone adoption expanded,
operator employment expanded with it: historical summaries report approximately 88,000 female
telephone operators in 1910, 178,000 in 1920, and 235,000 in 1930. [6]
Automatic switching progressively transferred the connection function from people into network
infrastructure. The telephone did not disappear. Communication demand did not disappear. A human
function embedded inside the network became less necessary.
Modern occupational categories are not directly comparable with the early twentieth-century workforce,
but the contrast is illustrative: the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics estimated about 4,600 workers in the
narrower “telephone operators” category in May 2023. [7] The point is not a one-to-one historical count;
classification changed. The point is that the network retained and expanded its function while much of
the manual connection function migrated into infrastructure.
When did displacement begin: at layoff, at automated switching, or when the network
became capable of inheriting the operator’s function?
8. CASE: THE INTERNET AS AN INHERITED CAPABILITY SURFACE
The internet demonstrates infrastructure inheritance at planetary scale. The International
Telecommunication Union estimated that about 6 billion people, 74 percent of the world population, were
online in 2025. [8]
The labor point here is not that “the internet caused displacement.” The narrower point is that later
technologies inherited an already-deployed distribution layer. Online retail, streaming, cloud computing,
remote work, social platforms, software-as-a-service, and generative AI services did not have to
construct independent global delivery networks from zero.
The internet altered the feasible future by providing a common infrastructure through which later
capabilities could expand. This is infrastructure inheritance, one of the conditions for Seed Adoption.

This is not offered as evidence that each associated occupation disappeared. It is an illustration of
functional compression: capabilities can migrate into a common platform even when employment
consequences differ by industry. Product consolidation and labor displacement are not identical, but
consolidation can alter which functions remain economically distinct.
10. CASE: CAMERAS FROM RECORDING TO INTERPRETATION
The capability trajectory below is sourced to a specific federal audit [9]. The connection between that capability
trajectory and labor-market displacement in law enforcement is not independently evidenced here and should be
read as analysis built on a sourced capability finding, not as a sourced displacement finding.
At its simplest, a camera records and a human reviews. The technology assists observation but does not
replace interpretation.
Contemporary surveillance systems can do more. A December 2024 U.S. Government Accountability
Office review found selected Department of Homeland Security law-enforcement agencies using more
than 20 types of detection, observation, and monitoring technologies, including facial recognition and
automated license-plate readers, and using analytic software — including AI-based tools — to enhance
those systems. [9]
That does not establish that cameras are displacing police officers. Law enforcement includes physical
intervention, investigation, judgment, legal authority, interpersonal interaction, emergency response,
conflict management, and accountability. But it does establish a capability trajectory in which some
observation and correlation functions move deeper into infrastructure.
That produces both a labor question and a governance question.
 Labor: What happens when technology absorbs repetitive, observational, or safer portions of a role
while people increasingly retain difficult, exceptional, or dangerous work?
 Governance: Who controls evidence capable of contradicting the institution using the evidence
system?
If the same authority controls collection, access, retention, interpretation, deletion, malfunction
classification, and evidentiary presentation, the system is not merely an automation tool. It is also an
evidence-custody architecture.
11. CASE: INDUSTRIAL AND WAREHOUSE AUTOMATION
Industrial automation often advances through recognizable stages: human execution, machine
assistance, automated repetition, human supervision, multi-machine coordination, and eventually
greater concentration of human work in exceptions, maintenance, safety, integration, and abnormal
events.
The U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics projects that e-commerce will continue to increase demand for
warehousing and logistics, while also expecting relatively weaker employment growth in warehousing

because firms are adopting warehouse-management systems, automated guided vehicles, robots, and
AI-based systems. BLS explicitly states that the productivity gains from these technologies are expected
to limit labor demand. [10]
This is important because it demonstrates that sector growth and function compression can coexist. A
warehouse can remain busy, output can rise, and jobs can still exist while the human labor required per
unit of output declines.
The building does not have to become empty for displacement to occur at the task or
function level.
12. CASE STUDY: DATA-CENTER CONSTRUCTION
I encountered the Seed Adoption problem from inside the infrastructure itself.
I worked construction on a data center. At that moment, AI infrastructure was not taking work from me. It
was creating work for me. Human beings were needed to construct the physical environment supporting
computation: concrete, electrical systems, cooling infrastructure, networking, fiber, steel, logistics,
equipment systems, roads, power distribution, and the facilities themselves.
The scale of the build-out is material. In April 2026, the International Energy Agency reported that capital
expenditure by five large technology companies exceeded $400 billion in 2025 and was expected to rise
by a further 75 percent in 2026, driven by data-center investment and AI infrastructure. [11]
That produces a paradox. The same infrastructure creating construction employment today provides
computational capacity for technologies capable of reducing human labor requirements elsewhere
tomorrow. If increasingly capable AI systems become integrated with robotics and autonomous
machinery, some automation pressure can eventually return to physical industries, including parts of
construction.
That outcome is not guaranteed. The pathway is the point.
A technology can create employment during construction of the infrastructure required
for its later capability expansion.
Creation and displacement are therefore not opposites. They can exist at different stages of the same
system.
13. CASE: ARTIFICIAL INTELLIGENCE AND COGNITIVE WORK
Artificial intelligence moves automation farther upstream into cognitive workflows: writing, coding,
translation, documentation, customer support, research, planning, design, scheduling, administration,
analysis, and decision support.

global employment falls into its highest exposure category. The ILO’s central conclusion is that, because

That restraint is essential to Seed Adoption. Exposure is not displacement. Technical possibility is not
labor-market consequence. But the boundary of technically automatable human function is moving, and
cognitive automation can increasingly connect with physical automation.
Governance belongs in the gap between what a system can become capable of doing
and what society has actually authorized it to do.
14. PATH DEPENDENCE IS NOT INEVITABILITY
Seed Adoption does not mean Generation One causes Generation Six. It means Generation One can
change the conditions under which Generation Six becomes possible.
Early adoption can lower later deployment friction by establishing hardware, standards, data, user
habits, institutional legitimacy, legal acceptance, procurement systems, capital commitments,
organizational workflows, supporting industries, and public familiarity.
That is path dependence, not prophecy. A Seed Adoption finding should therefore be interpreted as a
governance signal: later capability expansion may inherit conditions that make new scrutiny less likely
even as consequences become larger.
15. INFRASTRUCTURE LOCK-IN AND THE REVERSIBILITY TEST
Technological systems accumulate inertia. Hardware is purchased. Software is integrated. Employees
are trained. Contracts are signed. Regulations adapt. Buildings are constructed. Data accumulates.
Supporting industries form. Organizations redesign workflows. Alternatives can lose investment.
Eventually the question changes from “Should we adopt this?” to “How could we operate without it?”
Reversibility is therefore a second axis of Seed Adoption. A system becomes more consequential when
later withdrawal becomes materially harder than initial adoption.
1. Can the organization still operate without the system?
2. Do workers still retain the capability the technology absorbed?
3. Are viable alternatives still available?
4. Has physical or digital infrastructure been reorganized around the technology?
5. Has institutional knowledge migrated into the system?

6. Would removal create major continuity loss?
7. Has the surrounding economy or service become dependent on it?
8. Can later capability generations be rejected without destabilizing the current system?
Substitution potential + reversibility loss = rising governance importance.
16. THE WORKER CAN HELP BUILD THE THING THAT CHANGES
THE WORK
Workers often participate in technological transition themselves. They install automation, train software,
correct models, create data, build data centers, operate machines, document procedures, report failures,
improve workflows, and teach organizations how new systems should be used.
None of this makes workers irrational. If a tool makes today’s work easier, adopting it can be entirely
reasonable.
Acceptance of present assistance does not imply acceptance of every future
substitution enabled by the same infrastructure.
“Help me perform this function” and “build a system in which this function no longer requires me” are
different governance decisions even when they occur on the same technical lineage.
17. WHO RECEIVES THE BENEFIT?
Displacement cannot be evaluated through employment counts alone. Suppose technology allows the
same output with half the human labor. The gain can be distributed in different ways.
 Workers can receive shorter workweeks, higher wages, safer conditions, less repetitive labor, or more
capable tools.
 Consumers can receive lower prices, faster service, greater availability, or better products.
 Organizations can receive higher output, lower costs, increased reliability, and reduced staffing
requirements.
 Owners and investors can receive a larger share of productivity gains.
 Employment can decline in some functions while new occupations emerge elsewhere.
Technology creates capability. Systems determine distribution.
A complete analysis must therefore ask who captures the benefit, who carries transition cost, who gains
authority, who loses bargaining power, who becomes dependent, and who becomes optional.

18. SEED ADOPTION REVIEW GATE
If Seed Adoption is to be more than an observation, it needs a governance handoff. The following review
gate is intentionally bounded: it identifies when enhanced review should begin. It is not a complete
implementation specification.
Gate A — Establish whether Seed Adoption is present
Use the three qualification dimensions from Section 3. If functional inheritance or infrastructure
inheritance is absent, no Seed Adoption finding is made. If both are present but later capability still
requires substantive new authorization, classify the system as a Seed Pathway. If both are present and
later capability can expand under materially reduced adoption friction, classify Seed Adoption as
PRESENT.

19. WHAT EVIDENCE SHOULD A REVIEW PRESERVE?
A governance-ready implementation should preserve a reconstructable record rather than relying on
retrospective narrative. At minimum, each material capability generation should produce a bounded
evidence record containing:
 Adopted capability and intended purpose.
 Human function being assisted or executed.
 Existing infrastructure, data, and workflow authority inherited by the new capability.
 What new authorization, if any, was required.
 Observed changes in task allocation, labor hours, staffing, role content, safety, and productivity.
 Available alternatives and the cost of reversal.
 Expected next-generation capability and known substitution potential.
 Decision authority responsible for approving expansion.
 Review date and trigger for reassessment.
This creates a handoff from the publication concept to an enforceable governance instrument. A future
Seed Adoption specification could formalize scoring, receipts, reassessment triggers, approval
boundaries, rollback, and independent review requirements.

20. COUNTERARGUMENTS AND LIMITS
“Technology has always created new jobs.” True. This is not a rebuttal. The automobile, telephone,
computer, internet, and automation systems created new occupations and industries. Seed Adoption
does not require permanent mass unemployment. It asks where human necessity moved and whether
later substitution inherited an upstream adoption decision.
“Every technology creates future possibilities, so every adoption is Seed Adoption.” No. A
technology must demonstrate functional inheritance, infrastructure inheritance, and materially reduced
adoption friction. A useful tool that does not create a substitution pathway fails the test.
“This confuses efficiency with displacement.” No. Efficiency becomes displacement-relevant only
when reduced human input changes the amount, composition, or necessity of labor demanded.
Productivity alone is not sufficient.
“We cannot govern every hypothetical future use.” Correct. Governance should be proportional.
Review intensity should rise as substitution becomes technically plausible, dependency increases,
reversibility declines, human capability decays, and downstream consequences become larger.
Governance should grow with evidence and consequence, not imagination alone.
“Historical cases are not proof that AI will follow the same path.” Correct. The cases establish
recurring mechanisms, not a deterministic forecast. Seed Adoption is a framework for asking whether
those mechanisms are present in a current system, not a claim that every technological transition ends
the same way.
21. GOVERNANCE ARRIVES TOO LATE
Our usual sequence often looks like this: technology adoption; infrastructure deployment; capital
investment; institutional reorganization; dependency; alternative decay; capability expansion;
human-function compression; visible worker displacement; public debate.
Then society asks what it should do about automation.
But by that point, large parts of the system may already be built. The earlier question is more useful:
What future capabilities does this adoption make easier, and what happens if those capabilities expand?
This does not require stopping innovation. It requires preserving lineage and refusing to treat capability
expansion as automatically authorized merely because an earlier, narrower capability was accepted.
22. TECHNOLOGY IS NOT THE ENEMY
Technology has repeatedly removed dangerous labor, reduced physical burden, increased productivity,
expanded communication, created occupations, improved medicine and transportation, increased
access to information, and made things possible that earlier generations could not accomplish.

History does not support the equation technology = unemployment. It also does not support the claim
that assistance has no displacement consequences until a worker receives a termination notice.
Technology redistributes human necessity.
Where that necessity moves, who controls the new system, who receives the gains, who absorbs losses,
whether alternatives remain viable, and whether later capability generations receive fresh authorization
are governance questions.
23. CONCLUSION
Seed Adoption does not claim that every useful technology eventually replaces its user. It claims
something narrower: technological displacement has lineage, and later capability generations can inherit
infrastructure and legitimacy from earlier ones.
A primitive technology can look harmless because it is harmless. An assistive system can genuinely help
workers because it does help them. A new industry can genuinely create employment because it does
create employment. None of those facts establish what later capability generations will do.
That is why technological governance cannot evaluate only present capability. It must also examine what
present adoption makes easier to build next, whether later generations can enter under inherited
authority, and whether society retains meaningful capacity to reverse course.
The technology that eventually replaces a function rarely enters society announcing
that it intends to replace the function. It enters by being useful.
The first generation may sell assistance. The last generation may no longer require the
person the first generation was built to assist.
By the time a technology takes your job, the decision that made your displacement
technically possible may be years old.
The governing question is therefore not simply whether technology will replace people.
At what point does assistance become infrastructure for substitution?

pathways across technologies. Where occupational classifications changed over time, figures are presented as
contextual evidence rather than direct like-for-like series.
Evidentiary status by section, for transparency: Sections 6, 7, 8, 11, 12, and 13 cite external, checkable sources for
their empirical claims [5–13]. Section 10 cites a sourced capability finding [9] but its connection to a labor-market
outcome is this paper’s analysis, not an independently sourced displacement claim. Section 9 (smartphones) is
